\chapter{Using the library}\label{ch:using}

Part~I built the methods from Taylor's theorem up. This chapter is for the reader with a problem: a function, a
point and a derivative to compute. It shows the call and its result, gives a table for choosing a method, works
through three engineering examples, shows how to check a result that comes without an error estimate, covers
user-defined number types and compile-time use, and ends with the pitfalls, each with its symptom and its cure.
\Cref{ch:api} is the exact reference; \cref{ch:tables} holds the tables.

\section{The call}\label{sec:using-call}

Everything is in one header, \code{<numerixx/deriv.hpp>}, in the namespace \code{nxx::deriv}. The library is
header-only and has no dependencies; the CMake target \code{numerixx::numerixx} adds the include path and requires
C++23. There is one function, \code{derivative}, in two forms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{derivative<M, N>(fn, x)} uses the method's default step;
  \item \code{derivative<M, N>(fn, x, h)} uses the step \code{h} you pass (for Ridders' method, the initial step).
\end{itemize}
The method \code{M} is a template argument: \code{Central<P>}, \code{Forward<P>}, \code{Backward<P>} or
\code{Ridders}, and \code{Central<2>} when it is left out. The derivative order N is 1 by default; it is an integer
from 1 to 4 or a value of the enumeration \code{order}, such as \code{order::second}. The number type T is deduced
from \code{x}, and a step of another type converts to it, so \code{0.01} is a valid step for a 50-digit \code{x}.
\code{fn} is any callable\mnote{A \emph{callable} is anything that \code{std::invoke} can call: a function, a
lambda, a function object, a member pointer.} that takes a T and returns something convertible to T. The call of
\cref{ch:why}, \code{derivative(H, T0)}, is the shortest form: \code{Central<2>}, the first derivative, the default
step. \Cref{sec:api-derivative} lists every form, with a compiled example of each.

A stencil calls \code{fn} once per node, so \code{Central<2>} costs two evaluations for $f'$ and three for $f''$;
Ridders' method costs two or four per level, with a median of \num{\valRiddersEvalsMedianFirst} for $f'$ and
\num{\valRiddersEvalsMedianThird} for $f'''$ in the random study of \cref{ch:ridders} (\cref{tab:cost}).

\section{The result}\label{sec:using-result}

\code{derivative} never throws. Its result is a \code{std::expected<Estimate<T>, error>}, which holds either an
estimate or the reason there is none. An \code{Estimate<T>} has two members, \code{value} and \code{abs_error}; the second is a
\code{std::optional<T>}, filled by Ridders' method and empty for the stencils. The one reason so far is
\code{error::non_finite}. The usual pattern is to test the result, then read it:
\snippet{07-using-expected}{expected-basics}
The central rule at $x = 0$ samples $\log(-h)$, which is NaN, so the first call fails. \code{error::non_finite}
covers every case in which no finite estimate exists: \code{x} is not finite; \code{fn} returned an infinite or NaN
value; a difference, term, sum or quotient would overflow; a point $x + k h$ would overflow; or $h$ is below the
resolution of $x$, so that $x + h$ rounds back to $x$ (\cref{sb:ulp}). The library documents that \code{error} may gain
values, so a \code{switch} on it needs a \code{default}.

Because the result is a \code{std::expected}, the standard monadic operations work on it. \code{transform} converts a
value and passes a failure through; \code{and_then} chains a second call that can fail itself:
\snippet{07-using-expected}{expected-chain}
Both lambdas take a \code{double}: an \code{Estimate<double>} converts to its value where a \code{double} is
expected. The conversion is convenient and has three traps, shown in the last snippet:
\snippet{07-using-expected}{estimate-pitfalls}
First, \code{auto} deduces \code{Estimate<double>}, and \code{std::println} does not apply the conversion: print
\code{e.value}. Second, \code{if (*r)} tests the value against zero, not whether \code{r} holds a result: the
derivative of $\cos x$ at 0 is exactly 0, a good result that tests false (Clang with \code{-Wconversion -Werror}
rejects the line). Third, \code{d.value_or(0.0)} does not compile, since no \code{double} converts to an
\code{Estimate<double>}; \code{d->abs_error.value_or(0.0)} does. And \code{int n = *r} truncates.

\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:using-examples}

\subsection{Newton's method for a temperature}\label{sec:using-newton}

An energy balance often gives the enthalpy of a stream and asks for its temperature: solve $\enth(T) = H^\ast$ for
$T$. Newton's method needs the slope $\deriv{\enth}{T} = \cp$, which \code{derivative} supplies. This is the classic
use of a derivative inside an iteration, so the method is the default \code{Central<2>}, applied to the thread's
enthalpy \code{H(T)}:
\snippet{07-using-newton}{newton}
\begin{marginfigure}
\centering
\input{figures/07-using-newton}
\caption{Newton's residual $\abs{\enth(T) - H^\ast}$ against the iteration, with the slope from \code{Central<2>}:
the correct digits double at each step until the residual reaches the rounding of $\enth$. Data
\code{data/newton.csv}.\label{fig:newton}}
\end{marginfigure}
Started at \SI{\valNewtonStart}{K} for the enthalpy of \SI{1000}{K}, it converges in \num{\valNewtonIterations}
iterations to within \SI{\valNewtonFinalError}{K} (measured, \cref{fig:newton}); the final residual,
\SI{\valNewtonFinalResidual}{\joule\per\mole}, is the rounding of $\enth$ itself. A slope that is off by a relative
$\delta$ adds a term of about $\delta$ times the error to each step,\cite{dennis1996} and a $\delta$ near
\num{\valCpDefaultRelError} is far too small to slow the convergence before rounding stops it. Each iteration costs
three evaluations of $\enth$: one for the residual and two for the slope. Ridders' method would cost
\num{\valCpRiddersEvals} evaluations per slope here and buy nothing the iteration can use.

\subsection{Sensitivities of a reaction rate}\label{sec:using-sensitivity}

A sensitivity is a derivative of a model output with respect to one input, the others held fixed. Take an Arrhenius
rate constant, $k = A\,\mathrm{e}^{-E_a/(\Rgas T)}$, with the illustrative values $A = \SI{e10}{\per\second}$,
$E_a = \SI{80}{\kilo\joule\per\mole}$ and $T = \SI{600}{K}$. The model is a function of three arguments, and
\code{derivative} differentiates a function of one: a lambda that captures the others makes one. With the model as
a function \code{rate(A, Ea, T)}:
\snippet{07-using-sensitivity}{sensitivity}
These are values to report, so the method is Ridders'. Against the closed forms $\partial k/\partial T = k
E_a/(\Rgas T^2)$ and $\partial k/\partial E_a = -k/(\Rgas T)$ the measured relative errors are
\num{\valSensitivityRateRelError} and \num{\valSensitivityEaRelError}, each below its estimate. Engineers usually
report the scaled sensitivity $\deriv{\ln k}{\ln E_a} = (E_a/k)\,\partial k/\partial E_a$, the relative change of $k$
per relative change of $E_a$, which is dimensionless; here it is \num{\valSensitivityScaled}, as the exact $-E_a/(\Rgas T)$:
a small relative error in the activation energy reaches the rate magnified by that factor.

A Jacobian is the same thing done once per parameter: a loop over the parameters whose lambda copies the parameter
vector, replaces entry $j$ and calls the model, with one Ridders call per column (snippet \code{jacobian} in
\code{code/07-using-sensitivity.cpp}). Its cost is the sum over the columns: \num{\valJacobianEvals} evaluations for
$(A, E_a)$, of which \num{\valJacobianEvalsA} for $A$ (the rate is linear in $A$, so the first extrapolated entry
agrees exactly with its parent and the method stops) and \num{\valJacobianEvalsEa} for $E_a$. The library has no
gradient of its own (\cref{sec:using-not}).

\subsection{A function with no formula: a friction factor}\label{sec:using-solver}

The thread's enthalpy has a closed-form derivative; most functions in practice do not. The Darcy friction factor
$\lambda$ of turbulent flow in a rough pipe follows from the Colebrook equation\cite{KEY-REQUESTED}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = -2 \log_{10}\left(\frac{10^{-4}}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\mathit{Re}\,\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)
  \label{eq:using-colebrook}
\end{equation}
for a relative roughness of $10^{-4}$, which has no closed-form solution: $\lambda(\mathit{Re})$ is computed by an inner
iteration, here bisection on $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$ to a relative tolerance $\noise$.
\snippet{07-using-solver}{implicit-function}
The output of such a solver is accurate to about its tolerance, not to machine epsilon: at
$\mathit{Re} = 10^5$ with $\noise = 10^{-10}$ it is \num{\valSolverFrictionNoise}
off, relative, and it changes in steps as $\mathit{Re}$ moves. That is the noisy function of \cref{sec:rounding-noise},
and its cure applies: the step $\noise^{1/3} s$ instead of the default $\epsmach^{1/3} s$.
\snippet{07-using-solver}{implicit-derivative}
The answer key is the implicit-function derivative of \cref{eq:using-colebrook} at its root, found to machine
precision by Newton's method. With the default step, \num{\valSolverDefaultStep} in units of $\mathit{Re}$, the
relative error is \num{\valSolverDefaultRelError}: the rounding branch, $\noise \abs{\lambda} / h$, at a step much too
small. With $h = \noise^{1/3}\,\mathit{Re}$, about \num{\valSolverFitStep}, it is \num{\valSolverFitRelError}, inside
the model's $\noise^{2/3} = \num{\valSolverModelFitRelError}$ (measured). Ridders' method returned a relative error of
\num{\valSolverDerivRelError} with a relative estimate of \num{\valSolverDerivRelEstimate}, so its estimate covered
the error here; but the estimate assumes $f$ accurate to $\epsmach$, and over \num{\valRiddersPointsPerFunction} random points of a
function with relative noise $10^{-8}$ it covered only \SI{\valNoiseEightRiddersCoverage}{\percent} of the cases
(\cref{sec:rounding-noise}). Pass the step; do not rely on the estimate.

The solver matters. A fixed-point iteration stopped at the same tolerance is smooth between the values of
$\mathit{Re}$ at which its iteration count changes, but off by up to its tolerance: Ridders' method on it was
\num{\valSolverFixedPointRiddersRelError} off with a relative estimate of \num{\valSolverFixedPointRiddersRelEstimate}
(measured). A method differentiates the function it is given; no estimate sees how far that is from the one you
meant.

\section{Is my derivative good enough?}\label{sec:using-check}

A stencil returns no error estimate. Four checks, in increasing cost, tell you whether to trust it. The first three
use the thread's $\cp$ at $T_0 = \SI{1000}{K}$ and count the decimal digits on which two values agree,
$-\log_{10}(\abs{a - b} / \abs{b})$, with a helper \code{digits(a, b)}.

\paragraph{Repeat with $2h$ and $h/2$.} While truncation dominates, halving the step quarters the error of
\code{Central<2>}; while rounding dominates, it doubles it. Values that agree to many digits at $h$, $2h$ and $h/2$ are
accurate to about that many:
\snippet{07-using-check}{check-by-steps}
Here they agree to \num{\valCheckStepsDigits} digits, and the value agrees with the closed form to
\num{\valCheckExactDigits} (measured). A kink or a jump inside the span does not survive this check: a jump $J$ adds
$J / (2h)$ to the central difference, which doubles as the step halves.

\paragraph{Use a rule of higher order.} \code{derivative<Central<4>>(H, T0)} has its own default step and a much
smaller error, so its agreement with \code{Central<2>}, \num{\valCheckOrderDigits} digits here, measures the error of
the second-order value.

\paragraph{Ask Ridders' method.} It gives an independent value with an estimate of its own error:
\snippet{07-using-check}{check-by-ridders}
Its estimate, \SI{\valCheckRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}, belongs to Ridders' value, not to the
stencil's. But when it is small, the difference $\abs{d - r} = \SI{\valCheckRiddersDiff}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}$
is the stencil's error to within it; the actual error is \SI{\valCheckStencilActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}.

\paragraph{Look at the absolute error where $f'$ is near zero.} Near a stationary point of $f$ few digits of $f'$ can
agree, because the error of the rule scales with $\abs{f}$, not with $\abs{f'}$. For $\sin x$ just above $\pi/2$
(the example takes $x$ to four decimals), \code{Central<2>} gives $f' = \num{\valCheckSineDerivative}$ with an absolute error of
\num{\valCheckSineAbsError}: a relative error of \num{\valCheckSineRelError}, yet the three values at $h$, $2h$ and
$h/2$ differ by \num{\valCheckSineSpread}, which is \num{\valCheckSineDigitsScale} digits of $\abs{f}/s$ and only
\num{\valCheckSineDigitsDerivative} of $f'$ (measured, \code{check-absolute} in \code{code/07-using-check.cpp}). Judge
such a derivative by its absolute error, in the units of $f/x$.

Finally, a physical check costs nothing: a heat capacity is positive, a reaction rate grows with temperature, a
pressure drop grows with the flow. A derivative of the wrong sign is wrong whatever the digits say.

\section{User-defined and multiprecision types}\label{sec:using-types}

\code{derivative} works on any type that satisfies the concept \code{nxx::real}: the built-in floating-point types, and
user types such as Boost.Multiprecision's numbers, with or without expression templates (\cref{sec:api-real} lists
the requirements). The algorithms use only $+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$, comparisons and \code{numeric_limits}, so a
50-digit type gets 50-digit derivatives:
\snippet{07-using-multiprecision}{multiprecision}
With \code{cpp_bin_float_50}, whose machine epsilon is \num{\valEpsMultiprecision}, \code{Central<6>} at its default
step is \num{\valMpCentralSixRelError} off, relative, and Ridders' method \num{\valMpRiddersRelError} with a relative
estimate of \num{\valMpRiddersRelEstimate} after \num{\valMpRiddersEvals} evaluations (measured on $\mathrm{e}^x$ at
1). \code{Central<2>} would stop at \num{\valMpCentralTwoRelError}: a wide type is where the higher orders pay
(\cref{tab:accuracy-matrix}). Two details of the snippet matter with such types, \code{-> mp50} on the lambda and
\code{mp50(*r)} in the arithmetic; \cref{sb:expression-templates} explains both.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:expression-templates]{Expression templates and user-defined real types}
\code{nxx::real<T>} asks for little: \code{std::numeric_limits<T>} specialised as bounded, inexact and non-integer
(so that \code{max()} is finite and \code{epsilon()} positive), construction from \code{int}, a total order, and
$+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$ and unary minus whose results \emph{convert} to T. They need not \emph{be} T, because
Boost.Multiprecision with expression templates returns an expression object from \code{a + b}: a small record of the
operation that refers to its operands and is evaluated when it is converted to a number. Exact types such as
rationals are rejected, since their epsilon is 0.

An expression object that outlives its operands refers to destroyed values. The library therefore never writes
\code{auto} for an arithmetic result: every intermediate is a \code{T const}, and the value of \code{fn} is converted
with \code{static_cast<T>} before any arithmetic, so \code{fn} may return an expression. User code needs the same care
in two places. A lambda that returns \code{exp(x)} for a Boost type may return an expression that refers to its
parameter; \code{-> T} makes it return a number. And Boost's operators are templates, whose argument deduction does
not consider the conversion of an \code{Estimate<T>} to T: \code{abs(*r - e)} does not compile, while
\code{abs(T(*r) - e)} and \code{r->value - e} do. Constants are written \code{T(2)}, never
\code{2.0}, inside the library, because a \code{double} literal would silently carry only 16 digits into a 50-digit
computation.
\end{sidebar}

\section{At compile time}\label{sec:using-constexpr}

\code{derivative} is \code{constexpr}: when \code{fn} can itself be evaluated at compile time, so can its derivative,
and the result can feed a \code{static_assert} or a constant table. With a step that is a power of two, $x^3$ gives
exact results in binary arithmetic, which makes the truncation error of \cref{ch:taylor} visible to the compiler:
\snippet{07-using-constexpr}{constexpr}
Failures are values at compile time too: the thread's fit, written to return NaN below its validity range, gives
\code{error::non_finite} for \code{Central<2>} at \SI{500}{K} and a value for \code{Forward<2>}, both checked by
\code{static_assert} in \code{code/07-using-constexpr.cpp}. The one exception is an overflow of the final quotient,
for example a fourth derivative with a tiny step: at run time it is \code{non_finite}, but GCC rejects it at compile
time. Most functions of \code{<cmath>}, \code{exp} and \code{log} among them, are not \code{constexpr} in C++23, so such an
\code{fn} is written with arithmetic only; the thread's enthalpy is.

The compiler and the processor compute the same value, provided the compiler neither contracts \code{a * b + c} into
a fused multiply-add nor uses extended precision; the build pins neither, and the identity is verified on the five
toolchains of the continuous integration (\cref{sec:guarantees-guarantees}). How the library avoids the arithmetic that
constant evaluation rejects is the subject of \cref{sb:constexpr-overflow}.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:constexpr-overflow]{Overflow guards and constant evaluation}
At run time an overflow gives an infinity and a failed check afterwards. In a constant expression it is worse: GCC
rejects any floating-point overflow and any NaN, Clang a NaN and a division by zero, so a failure would become a
compile error instead of an error value. The library therefore detects each failure before the arithmetic that would
produce it. With $K$ the largest node and $Q = \max(2K, \den)$, the input checks $h \le \fmax / 2Q$ and $\abs{x} \le
\fmax - Q h$ keep every point $x + k h$ at least about $K h$ below $\fmax$ (a NaN $x$ fails them too). Every
difference and partial sum is checked before it is formed: \code{sum_fits} compares $a/\beta + b/\beta$ with
$\fmax/\beta$, $\beta$ the radix, which is exact and cannot overflow, so the NaN of $\infty - \infty$ never arises.
\code{product_fits} decides exactly whether a term $c_k f_k$ is finite, and the rounding floors of Ridders' method
saturate at $\fmax$. The exception is the final quotient, with the repeated divisions by $h$ for $\dorder \ge 2$:
guarding it exactly would need overflow tests accurate to the last unit, so it is checked afterwards, which GCC
rejects in a constant expression. Overflow is detected, not avoided: halving the operands would avoid it, but halving
is not exact in a decimal type, and a step near $\fmax$ has no numerical meaning.
\end{sidebar}

\section{Pitfalls}\label{sec:using-pitfalls}

Each pitfall below gives a wrong or missing result without a crash. Symptom first, then the cure.

\paragraph{The step's scale.} The default step assumes that $f$ varies on the scale $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$ in your units.
\emph{Symptom:} at a large $\abs{x}$ with a narrow feature, Ridders' method or a smaller step disagrees with the
default; at a small $\abs{x}$ such as a mole fraction of $10^{-6}$, the step is larger than $x$ and the result is
\code{non_finite} or crosses a physical bound. \emph{Cure:} pass $h = \epsmach^{1/3} w$, with $w$ the scale on which
$f$ varies (\cref{fig:step-scale,fig:step-scale-small}).

\paragraph{A kink or a range break inside the span.} A property model is often piecewise: two fits joined at a
temperature, a phase boundary, an \code{if}. The published enthalpy of nitrogen uses one Shomate fit below
\SI{\valKinkJunction}{K} and another above, and the two do not meet: $\enth$ jumps by
\SI{\valKinkJumpEnthalpy}{\joule\per\mole} at \SI{\valKinkJunction}{K}.\cite{nist-webbook} Every method evaluates
this function without complaint, \SI{1}{mK} above the break:
\snippet{07-using-kink}{kink}
\begin{marginfigure}
\centering
\input{figures/07-using-kink}
\caption{Relative error of $\cp$ against the distance $\delta$ from the range break at \SI{\valKinkJunction}{K}.
Inside a method's span the error of a straddling stencil grows as $1/h$, whatever its order, and the rule returns it
without failing; for a kink rather than a jump it would be $\order(1)$ or $\order(h)$. Only Ridders' estimate (dashed)
shows it. Data \code{data/kink.csv}.\label{fig:kink}}
\end{marginfigure}
All three calls succeed. \code{Central<2>} is \num{\valKinkInsideCentralRelError} off, relative (the jump over $2h$),
and \code{Central<4>} \num{\valKinkInsideCentralFourRelError}: a higher order does not cure a function that is not
smooth. Ridders' value is \num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelError} off, but its estimate,
\SI{\valKinkInsideRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} or \num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelEstimate} of the
value, is far above its usual size (\num{\valKinkOutsideRiddersRelEstimate} of the value away from the break): its extrapolation assumes an error expansion in even
powers of $h$, which the break destroys, so the tableau's entries disagree. At \SI{\valKinkOutsideDelta}{K} from the
break, outside every span, all three errors are below \num{\valKinkOutsideCentralRelError} (measured,
\cref{fig:kink}). Evaluable is not smooth. \emph{Symptom:} a large estimate from Ridders' method, or stencil values
that change with the step. \emph{Cure:} differentiate the branch you mean, with \code{Forward<P>} above a break or
\code{Backward<P>} below it, or the branch's own function.

\paragraph{Aliasing at a large $\abs{x}$.} A function that oscillates on a scale far below $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$ can lie
about its slope at every sample: $\sin x$ near $x = 10^4 \pi$ from the default steps is the documented case
(\cref{sb:aliasing}). \emph{Cure:} a step on the oscillation's scale.

\paragraph{A noisy function.} A solver's output or a fitted table is accurate to $\noise$, not to $\epsmach$.
\emph{Symptom:} the error with the default step is far above $\epsmach^{2/3}$, and Ridders' estimate is optimistic.
\emph{Cure:} the step $\noise^{1/3} s$ (\cref{sec:using-solver}, \cref{sec:rounding-noise}).

\paragraph{A domain edge.} Ridders' method restarts below a step at which $f$ is not finite, but it needs the nearest
bad point a minimum distance away: a value was returned for $\log x$ down to $x = 2^{-\num{\valRiddersEdgeLimitExponent}}$
with its default first-derivative step (measured, \cref{fig:ridders-edge}). Exactly at an edge, use \code{Forward<P>}
or \code{Backward<P>}. And a fit can be evaluated outside its range: wrap it so that it returns NaN there, as the
example of \cref{sec:stencils-one-sided} does, so that a sample outside the range fails instead of returning a number
that means nothing.

\paragraph{The fourth derivative in float.} It is mostly rounding noise: \num{\valFloatFourthCentralRelError} for
\code{Central<2>} on $\mathrm{e}^x$ (median, \cref{fig:order-accuracy}). The library warns at compile time
(\cref{sec:api-warning}). \emph{Cure:} double.

\paragraph{\code{auto} on the result.} \code{auto v = *d;} is an \code{Estimate}, and \code{std::format} and Boost's
operators do not see its conversion (\cref{sec:using-result}). \emph{Cure:} \code{d->value}, or \code{double v = *d}.

\paragraph{One step for several orders.} The default step differs per method and order, from $\epsmach^{1/3} s$ for
$f'$ to $\epsmach^{1/6} s$ for $f''''$ with \code{Central<2>} (\cref{tab:default-steps}), because the rounding error
grows as $\epsmach / h^N$. A step tuned for $f'$ leaves nothing of $f''''$. \emph{Cure:} in generic code that varies
the order, use the form without a step.

\section{What the library does not do}\label{sec:using-not}

\code{nxx::deriv} differentiates a function, not a table. Tabulated data must be fitted or interpolated first, and the
derivative is then that of the fit: a linear interpolant has a piecewise-constant derivative and a kink at every
node, so a smooth fit or a spline is the place to start. Several features are deferred, and named here so that their
absence is not mistaken for an oversight: gradients, Jacobians and Hessians (a loop of calls, as in
\cref{sec:using-sensitivity}, does the job meanwhile); several orders from shared evaluations; a tolerance at which
Ridders' method stops early; derivatives above the fourth; stencils supplied by the user; a step chosen from an
estimate of the noise of $f$;\cite{more2012,gill1983} and the complex step and automatic differentiation, which need
more of $f$ than a black box (\cref{sb:alternatives}). \Cref{sec:guarantees-deferred} lists them with their status.
