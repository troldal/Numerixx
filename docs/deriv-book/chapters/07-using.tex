\chapter{Using the library}\label{ch:using}

This chapter is for the reader with a problem: a function, a point and a derivative to compute. It covers getting
and calling the library, reading and checking the result, choosing a method, three engineering examples, other number
types, compile-time use and the pitfalls. \Cref{ch:api} is the exact reference; \cref{ch:tables} holds the tables.

\section{The call}\label{sec:using-call}

\paragraph{Getting the library.} \nxx{} is header-only and has no dependencies. It ships no install rule and no
\code{find_package} configuration, so a CMake project adds it as a subdirectory, with \code{add_subdirectory}, CPM or
\code{FetchContent}; the target \code{numerixx::numerixx} adds the include path and requires C++23. With
\code{FetchContent}, at a pinned commit or tag:
\cmakefile{getting-started}
The library's tests and this book's examples run, in the continuous integration, on GCC~16, on Clang~22 with
libc++, on MSVC and clang-cl of Visual Studio~2026, and on Emscripten~6.0.10. These are the toolchains tested, not
minimum versions.

\paragraph{One function.} Everything is in \code{<numerixx/deriv.hpp>}, in the namespace \code{nxx::deriv}. There is
one function, \code{derivative}, in two forms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{derivative<M, N>(fn, x)} uses the method's default step;
  \item \code{derivative<M, N>(fn, x, h)} uses the step \code{h} you pass (for Ridders' method, the initial step).
\end{itemize}
The method \code{M} is a template argument: \code{Central<P>}, \code{Forward<P>}, \code{Backward<P>} or
\code{Ridders}, and \code{Central<2>} when it is left out. The derivative order N is 1 by default; it is an integer
from 1 to 4 or a value of the enumeration \code{order}, such as \code{order::second}. The number type T is deduced
from \code{x}, and a step of another type converts to it, so \code{0.01} is a valid step for a 50-digit \code{x}; for
a \code{float} \code{x} write \code{1e-3f}, since a \code{double} step is diagnosed under \code{-Wconversion} unless
\code{float} holds it exactly. \code{fn} is any callable\mnote{A \emph{callable} is anything \code{std::invoke} can
call: a function, a lambda, a function object, or a pointer to a const member function of T itself. For a member
function of your model, capture the object: \code{[&m](double t) { return m.H(t); }}.} that takes a T and returns
something convertible
to T. The call of \cref{ch:why}, \code{derivative(H, T0)}, is the shortest form: \code{Central<2>}, the first
derivative, the default step. \Cref{sec:api-derivative} shows one call of each form, compiled.

A stencil calls \code{fn} once per node, so \code{Central<2>} costs two evaluations for $f'$ and three for $f''$;
Ridders' method costs two or four per level, with a median of \num{\valRiddersEvalsMedianFirst} for $f'$ and
\num{\valRiddersEvalsMedianThird} for $f'''$ in the random study of \cref{ch:ridders} (\cref{tab:cost}).

\section{The result}\label{sec:using-result}

The library itself throws nothing: a failure is a value. An exception thrown by \code{fn} or by the operations of T
passes through unchanged, and \code{derivative} is not \code{noexcept}. The result holds either an estimate or the
reason there is none, as a \code{std::expected<Estimate<T>, error>}. An \code{Estimate<T>} has two members,
\code{value} and \code{abs_error}, a \code{std::optional<T>} filled by Ridders' method only. The one reason so far is
\code{error::non_finite}. Test the result, then read it:

\snippet{07-using-expected}{expected-basics}
The central rule at $x = 0$ samples $\log(-h)$, which is NaN, so the first call fails. \code{error::non_finite}
covers every case in which no finite estimate exists: \code{x} is not finite; \code{fn} returned an infinite or NaN
value; a difference, term, sum or quotient would overflow; a point $x + k h$ would overflow; or $h$ is below the
resolution of $x$ (\cref{sb:ulp}). \code{error} may gain values, so a \code{switch} on it needs a \code{default}. It
is formattable: \code{std::println("{}", d.error())} writes the enumerator's name, \code{non_finite}. To find
the cause, evaluate \code{fn} at $x \pm h$ yourself and compare $h$ with the spacing of the numbers at $x$.

The member functions \code{transform} and \code{and_then} of \code{std::expected} apply a function only when there is
a value, and pass a failure through; \code{and_then} chains a second call that can fail itself:
\needspace{13\baselineskip}% the listing in one piece (stage-6 layout: two comment lines were stranded)
\snippet{07-using-expected}{expected-chain}
Both lambdas take a \code{double}: an \code{Estimate<double>} converts to its value where a \code{double} is
expected. The conversion is convenient and has its traps, shown in the next snippet:
\snippet{07-using-expected}{estimate-pitfalls}
First, \code{auto} deduces \code{Estimate<double>}, and \code{std::println} does not apply the conversion: print
\code{e.value}. Second, \code{if (*r)} tests the value against zero, not whether \code{r} holds a result: the
derivative of $\cos x$ at 0 is exactly 0, a good result that tests false. Clang with \code{-Wconversion -Werror}
rejects that line; GCC accepts it without a warning. Third, \code{r.value()} is the accessor of \code{std::expected},
not the estimate's member: it compiles, converts to \code{double} and works until the first failure, where it throws
\code{std::bad_expected_access} (or terminates a program built without exceptions). Read \code{r->value} after the
test. Fourth, \code{r.value_or(0.0)} does not compile, since no \code{double} converts to an \code{Estimate<double>};
\code{r->abs_error.value_or(0.0)} does. And \code{int n = *r} truncates, with a \code{-Wfloat-conversion} warning on
GCC and Clang. The rest of the book reads a result as \code{d->value} and Ridders' estimate as \code{*r->abs_error}.

\paragraph{Did it work, and is it right?} \code{if (d)} answers the first question only. A step on the wrong scale
(\cref{sec:rounding-scale}), a function less accurate than $\epsmach$ (\cref{sec:rounding-noise}) or a break within a
step of $x$ (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}) give a wrong value without a failure; \cref{sec:using-check} shows how to
check one. Ridders' method also returns a value whose own estimate says it is poor, near a bad point or at the
resolution of $x$. Before a Ridders result is used where it matters, compare its estimate with the error you can
accept, in two tolerances:
\needspace{15\baselineskip}% the listing in one piece (stage-6 layout: its last three lines were stranded)
\snippet{07-using-expected}{ridders-check}
For a value reported to $n$ significant figures, \code{rtol} is $10^{-n}$; \code{atol}, the error you can accept in
the derivative's units, decides near a zero of $f'$ (\cref{sec:using-check}). Inside an iteration no check is needed
(\cref{sec:using-newton}). The check says that the estimate is small, not that the value is right where the estimate
is blind, as at a kink (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}).

\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:using-examples}

\subsection{Newton's method for a temperature}\label{sec:using-newton}

An energy balance often gives the enthalpy of a stream and asks for its temperature: solve $\enth(T) = H^\ast$ for
$T$. Newton's method needs the slope $\deriv{\enth}{T} = \cp$, which \code{derivative} supplies. This is the classic
use of a derivative inside an iteration, so the method is the default \code{Central<2>}, applied to the thread's
enthalpy \code{H(T)}. A failed slope and a failure to converge are both returned as errors, so a caller cannot take an
unconverged temperature for a solution:
\snippet{07-using-newton}{newton}
Started at \SI{\valNewtonStart}{K} for the enthalpy of \SI{1000}{K}, it converges in \num{\valNewtonIterations}
iterations to within \SI{\valNewtonFinalError}{K} (measured, \cref{fig:newton}); the final residual,
\SI{\valNewtonFinalResidual}{\joule\per\mole}, is the rounding of $\enth$ itself. A slope that is off by a relative
$\delta$ adds a term of about $\delta$ times the error to each step (Dennis and Schnabel,\cite{dennis1996}
Chapter 5), and a $\delta$ near
\num{\valCpDefaultRelError} is far too small to slow the convergence before rounding stops it. Each iteration costs
three evaluations of $\enth$; Ridders' method would cost \num{\valCpRiddersEvals} per slope and buy nothing the
iteration can use.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivities and a Jacobian}\label{sec:using-sensitivity}

A sensitivity is a derivative of a model output with respect to one input, the others held fixed. Take an Arrhenius
rate constant, $k = A\,\mathrm{e}^{-E_a/(\Rgas T)}$, with the illustrative values $A = \SI{e10}{\per\second}$,
$E_a = \SI{80}{\kilo\joule\per\mole}$ and $T = \SI{600}{K}$, as a function \code{rate(A, Ea, T)} of three
arguments. \code{derivative} differentiates a function of one; a lambda that captures the other two makes one:
\snippet{07-using-sensitivity}{sensitivity}
These are values to report, so the method is Ridders'. Against the closed forms $\partial k/\partial T = k
E_a/(\Rgas T^2)$ and $\partial k/\partial E_a = -k/(\Rgas T)$ the measured relative errors are
\num{\valSensitivityRateRelError} and \num{\valSensitivityEaRelError}, each below its estimate. Engineers usually
report the scaled sensitivity $\deriv{\ln k}{\ln E_a} = (E_a/k)\,\partial k/\partial E_a$, the relative change of $k$
per relative change of $E_a$, which is dimensionless; here it is \num{\valSensitivityScaled}, as the exact
$-E_a/(\Rgas T)$: a small relative error in the activation energy reaches the rate magnified by that factor. The
sensitivities to all the parameters are the same call in a loop, whose lambda replaces one parameter and keeps the
rest:
\snippet{07-using-sensitivity}{jacobian}
The cost is the sum over the parameters: \num{\valJacobianEvals} evaluations, of which \num{\valJacobianEvalsA} for
$A$ (the rate is linear in $A$, so the first extrapolated entry agrees exactly with its parent and the method stops)
and \num{\valJacobianEvalsEa} for $E_a$.

\paragraph{The Jacobian of a set of balances.} Newton's method for several unknowns needs the Jacobian of several
residuals, which a model usually returns from one call. Take an energy balance $r_1 = n\,\enth(T) - Q$ and an
ideal-gas pressure $r_2 = n \Rgas T / V - P$ in the unknowns $T$ and $n$, at $T = \SI{1000}{K}$, $n = \SI{2}{mol}$,
$V = \SI{1}{\metre\cubed}$, with $Q$ and $P$ such that both vanish there. The library differentiates a function of
one argument with one value, so it supports one way today: one \code{derivative} call per entry, on a lambda that
evaluates the whole model with one unknown varied, $m \times n$ calls each costing two model evaluations with
\code{Central<2>}: \num{\valJacobianBalanceCallEvals} model evaluations here, against
\num{\valJacobianBalanceHandEvals} by hand (snippet \code{jacobian-by-calls} in \code{code/07-using-jacobian.cpp}). For an expensive model, apply the central difference of \cref{ch:taylor} to the
whole vector yourself, two model calls per unknown, as in the loop of \code{jacobian(r, x)} in the same file, which
fills \code{J[i][j]} $= \partial r_i / \partial x_j$:
\snippet{07-using-jacobian}{jacobian-balances}
Here that is \num{\valJacobianBalanceHandEvals} model calls, with steps of \SI{\valJacobianBalanceStepT}{K} and
\SI{\valJacobianBalanceStepN}{mol}, and every entry is within \num{\valJacobianBalanceHandMaxRelError} of the closed
forms $n \cp$, $\enth$, $n \Rgas / V$ and $\Rgas T / V$ (measured; \num{\valJacobianBalanceCallMaxRelError} by the
calls). The loop is the formula without the library's checks: it neither detects a non-finite residual nor divides
by the distance actually evaluated, so test the residuals it returns. Several outputs from shared evaluations remain
a deferred feature (\cref{sec:using-not}).

\subsection{A function with no formula: a friction factor}\label{sec:using-solver}

The thread's enthalpy has a closed-form derivative; most functions in practice do not. The Darcy friction factor
$\lambda$ of turbulent flow in a rough pipe follows from the Colebrook equation, in its common
form,\cite{colebrook1939}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}} = -2 \log_{10}\left(\frac{10^{-4}}{3.7} + \frac{2.51}{\mathit{Re}\,\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)
  \label{eq:using-colebrook}
\end{equation}
for a relative roughness of $10^{-4}$, with no closed form: $\lambda(\mathit{Re})$ is computed by an inner iteration,
here bisection on $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$ to a relative tolerance $\noise$.
\snippetheader{colebrook}{implicit-function}
The output of such a solver is accurate to about its tolerance, not to machine epsilon: at
$\mathit{Re} = 10^5$ with $\noise = 10^{-10}$ it is \num{\valSolverFrictionNoise}
off, relative, and it changes in steps as $\mathit{Re}$ moves. That is the noisy function of \cref{sec:rounding-noise},
and its cure applies: the step $\noise^{1/3} s$ instead of the default $\epsmach^{1/3} s$.
\snippet{07-using-solver}{implicit-derivative}
The fitted step is the one to use; the default step and Ridders' estimate both assume $f$ accurate to $\epsmach$. The
answer key is the implicit-function derivative of \cref{eq:using-colebrook} at its root, found to machine precision by
Newton's method. With $h = \noise^{1/3}\,\mathit{Re}$, about \num{\valSolverFitStep}, the relative error is
\num{\valSolverFitRelError}, inside the model's $\noise^{2/3} = \num{\valSolverModelFitRelError}$ (measured); with
the default step, \num{\valSolverDefaultStep} in units of $\mathit{Re}$, it is \num{\valSolverDefaultRelError}, the
rounding branch $\noise \abs{\lambda} / h$ at a step much too small. Ridders' estimate happened to cover its error of
\num{\valSolverDerivRelError} here, but over \num{\valNoiseRiddersCases} random points of a function with relative
noise $10^{-8}$ it covered only \SI{\valNoiseEightRiddersCoverage}{\percent} of the cases (\cref{sec:rounding-noise}).
Here $\noise$ is known, because the tolerance is on the output itself. When a solver's test is on a residual
instead, measure $\noise$ with the recipe of \cref{sec:rounding-noise}: on this solver it returns
\num{\valNoiseEstimateFriction} relative (the actual deviation is \num{\valNoiseEstimateFrictionActual}) from
\num{\valNoiseEstimateEvals} evaluations.

A solver can also be smooth but wrong by its tolerance, and then no method and no estimate can see it: Ridders' method
on a fixed-point iteration stopped at the same tolerance was \num{\valSolverFixedPointRiddersRelError} off with a
relative estimate of only \num{\valSolverFixedPointRiddersRelEstimate} (measured). Converge the inner solver well
below the accuracy you want of the derivative, or differentiate the converged equations implicitly.

\section{Is my derivative good enough?}\label{sec:using-check}

A stencil returns no error estimate. Four checks, in increasing cost, tell you whether to trust it. The first three
use the thread's $\cp$ at $T_0 = \SI{1000}{K}$ and count the decimal digits on which two values agree,
$-\log_{10}(\abs{a - b} / \abs{b})$, with a helper \code{digits(a, b)}.

\paragraph{Repeat with $2h$ and $h/2$.} While truncation dominates, halving the step quarters the error of
\code{Central<2>}; while rounding dominates, it doubles it. Values that agree to many digits at $h$, $2h$ and $h/2$ are
accurate to about that many. \code{default_step<M, N>(x)} returns the step that the call without one uses
(\cref{sec:api-derivative}):
\needspace{16\baselineskip}% the listing in one piece (stage-6 layout)
\snippet{07-using-check}{check-by-steps}
Here they agree to \num{\valCheckStepsDigits} digits, and the value agrees with the closed form to
\num{\valCheckExactDigits} (measured). A jump inside the span does not survive this check: a jump $J$ adds $J / (2h)$
to the central difference, which doubles as the step halves. A kink at $x$ does: every step returns the mean of the
two slopes (\cref{sec:ridders-trust}).

\paragraph{Use a rule of higher order.} \code{Central<4>}, with its own default step and a much smaller error, agrees
with \code{Central<2>} to \num{\valCheckOrderDigits} digits here, which measures the latter's error.


\paragraph{Ask Ridders' method.} It gives an independent value with an estimate of its own error:
\snippet{07-using-check}{check-by-ridders}
Its estimate, \SI{\valCheckRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}, belongs to Ridders' value, not to the
stencil's. But when it is small, the difference $\abs{d - r} = \SI{\valCheckRiddersDiff}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}$
is the stencil's error to within Ridders' own error, \SI{\valCpRiddersActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} here, below
its estimate; the stencil's actual error is \SI{\valCheckStencilActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}.

\paragraph{Where $f'$ is near zero, judge the absolute error.} Near a stationary point of $f$ few digits of $f'$ can
agree although the derivative is accurate, because the error of the rule scales with $\abs{f}$, not with $\abs{f'}$.
For $\sin x$ just above $\pi/2$, \code{Central<2>} gives $f' = \num{\valCheckSineDerivative}$ with an absolute error
of only \num{\valCheckSineAbsError}, in units of $f$ per unit $x$, while the three values at $h$, $2h$ and $h/2$
agree in only \num{\valCheckSineDigitsDerivative} digits of the small slope (measured, \code{check-absolute} in
\code{code/07-using-check.cpp}). Compare the spread with $\abs{f} / \max(\abs{x}, 1)$, not with $f'$.

Finally, a physical check costs nothing: a heat capacity is positive, a reaction rate grows with temperature; a
derivative of the wrong sign is wrong whatever the digits say.

\section{User-defined and multiprecision types}\label{sec:using-types}

\code{derivative} works on any type that satisfies the concept \code{nxx::real}, which includes the
\code{numeric_limits} members the library reads: the built-in floating-point types, Boost.Multiprecision's numbers,
or a type of your own (\cref{sb:expression-templates}; \cref{sec:api-real} gives the list). The algorithms use only
$+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$, comparisons and \code{numeric_limits}, so a 50-digit type gets 50-digit derivatives:
\snippet{07-using-multiprecision}{multiprecision}
With \code{cpp_bin_float_50}, whose machine epsilon is \num{\valEpsMultiprecision}, \code{Central<6>} at its default
step is \num{\valMpCentralSixRelError} off, relative, and Ridders' method \num{\valMpRiddersRelError} with a relative
estimate of \num{\valMpRiddersRelEstimate} after \num{\valMpRiddersEvals} evaluations (measured on $\mathrm{e}^x$ at
1). \code{Central<2>} would stop at \num{\valMpCentralTwoRelError}: a wide type is where the higher orders pay
(\cref{tab:accuracy-matrix}). Two rules apply with every Boost type. In arithmetic, write \code{mp50(*r)} or
\code{r->value}: Boost's operators do not see the conversion of an \code{Estimate}. And give a lambda an explicit
\code{-> mp50}: the library converts a result at once, so an expression
over the parameter alone is safe, but one over the lambda's own locals would refer to destroyed values
(\cref{sb:expression-templates}); the explicit type is always safe.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:expression-templates]{Expression templates and user-defined real types}
\code{nxx::real<T>} asks for \code{std::numeric_limits<T>} specialised as bounded, inexact and non-integer, with
the \code{radix}, \code{digits}, \code{epsilon()}, \code{max()} and \code{denorm_min()} the algorithms read;
construction from \code{int}, copying, a total order, and $+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$ and unary minus whose results
\emph{convert} to T. A type that lacks one of these fails at the concept, at the call.
\Cref{sec:api-real} quotes a minimal type that provides exactly these members. Exact types
such as rationals are rejected, since \code{numeric_limits} marks them exact (and unbounded): their epsilon is 0 and
they have no largest value, so neither the step nor the overflow checks would mean anything.

The results of the operators need not \emph{be} T: Boost.Multiprecision with expression templates (\code{et_on})
returns from \code{a + b} an expression object that refers to its operands, which dangles once they are gone. The
library never writes \code{auto} for an arithmetic result and converts the value of \code{fn} with
\code{static_cast<T>} at once; \code{-> T} on a lambda does the same in user code. Boost's arithmetic operators are
templates that do not consider the conversion of an \code{Estimate<T>}: \code{abs(*r - e)} does not compile, with or
without expression templates, while \code{abs(T(*r) - e)} and \code{r->value - e} do.

\end{sidebar}

\section{At compile time}\label{sec:using-constexpr}

\code{derivative} is \code{constexpr}: when \code{fn} can itself be evaluated at compile time, so can its derivative,
and the result can feed a \code{static_assert} or a constant table. With a step that is a power of two, $x^3$ gives
exact results in binary arithmetic, which makes the truncation error of \cref{ch:taylor} visible to the compiler:
\snippet{07-using-constexpr}{constexpr}
Comparing the result with a value, as in \code{derivative(cube, 2.0, 0.25) == 12.0625}, compares \code{value} only,
through the conversion, and is false when the result is a failure; the same idiom with \code{!=} would pass on a
failure. Failures are values at compile time too: the thread's fit, written to return NaN below its validity range,
gives \code{error::non_finite} for \code{Central<2>} at \SI{500}{K} and a value for \code{Forward<2>}, both checked by
\code{static_assert} in \code{code/07-using-constexpr.cpp}. The one exception is an overflow of the final quotient,
for example a fourth derivative with a tiny step: at run time it is \code{non_finite}, but GCC rejects it at compile
time. Most of \code{<cmath>}, \code{exp} and \code{log} among them, is not \code{constexpr} in C++23, so such an
\code{fn} is written with arithmetic only; the thread's enthalpy is. GCC evaluates some of them in constant
expressions as an extension, which Clang and MSVC reject: test compile-time derivatives on every toolchain you ship.

The compiler and the processor compute the same value, provided the compiler neither contracts \code{a * b + c} into
a fused multiply-add nor uses extended precision; the build pins neither, and the identity is verified on the five
toolchains of the continuous integration (\cref{sec:guarantees-guarantees}). How the library avoids that arithmetic is
the subject of \cref{sb:constexpr-overflow}.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:constexpr-overflow]{Overflow guards and constant evaluation}
At run time an overflow gives an infinity and a failed check afterwards. In a constant expression it is worse: GCC
rejects any floating-point overflow and any NaN, Clang a NaN and a division by zero, so a failure would become a
compile error instead of an error value. The library therefore detects each failure before the arithmetic that would
produce it: the input checks keep every point $x + k h$ at least about $K h$ below $\fmax$, with $K$ the largest
node (a NaN $x$ fails them too); every difference and partial sum is tested before it is formed, by comparing
$a/\beta + b/\beta$ with $\fmax/\beta$ ($\beta$ the radix), which is exact and cannot overflow, so the NaN of
$\infty - \infty$ never arises; and the rounding floors of Ridders' method saturate at $\fmax$. The exception is the
final quotient, with the repeated divisions by $h$ for $\dorder \ge 2$: guarding it exactly would need overflow tests
accurate to the last unit, so it is checked afterwards, which GCC rejects in a constant expression.
\end{sidebar}

\section{Pitfalls}\label{sec:using-pitfalls}

Each pitfall below gives a wrong or missing result without a crash. Symptom first, then the cure.

\paragraph{The step's scale.} The default step assumes that $f$ varies on the scale $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$ in your units.
\emph{Symptom:} at a large $\abs{x}$ with a narrow feature, Ridders' method or a smaller step disagrees with the
default; at a small $\abs{x}$ such as a mole fraction of $10^{-6}$, the step is larger than $x$ and the result is
\code{non_finite} or crosses a physical bound. \emph{Cure:} pass $h = \epsmach^{1/3} w$, with $w$ the scale on which
$f$ varies (\cref{fig:step-scale,fig:step-scale-small}).

\paragraph{A kink or a range break inside the span.} A property model is often piecewise: two fits joined at a
temperature, a phase boundary, an \code{if}. The published enthalpy of nitrogen uses one Shomate fit below
\SI{\valKinkJunction}{K} and another above, and the two do not meet: $\enth$ jumps by
\SI{\valKinkJumpEnthalpy}{\joule\per\mole} at \SI{\valKinkJunction}{K}.\cite{nist-webbook} Every method evaluates
it without complaint \SI{1}{mK} above the break:

All three calls succeed. \code{Central<2>} is \num{\valKinkInsideCentralRelError} off, relative (the jump over $2h$),
and \code{Central<4>} \num{\valKinkInsideCentralFourRelError}: a higher order does not cure a function that is not
smooth. Ridders' value is \num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelError} off, but its estimate,
\SI{\valKinkInsideRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} or \num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelEstimate} of the
value, is far above its usual size (\num{\valKinkOutsideRiddersRelEstimate} of the value away from the break): its
extrapolation assumes an error expansion in even powers of $h$, which the break destroys, so the tableau's entries
disagree. At \SI{\valKinkOutsideDelta}{K} from the break, outside every span, all three errors are at most
\num{\valKinkOutsideCentralRelError} (measured, \cref{fig:kink}). Evaluable is not smooth. \emph{Symptom:} a large
estimate from Ridders' method, or stencil values that change with the step; a kink at $x$ shows no symptom at all:
central rules return the mean of the two slopes (\cref{sec:ridders-trust}). \emph{Cure:} know the branch
boundaries, and differentiate the branch you mean, with \code{Forward<P>} above a break or \code{Backward<P>} below
it, or the branch's own function.

\paragraph{A Ridders result that is a value but poor.} Near a bad point or at the resolution of $x$, Ridders'
method can return a value whose own estimate is a large fraction of it. \emph{Symptom:} \code{*r->abs_error} far
above the accuracy you need. \emph{Cure:} the check of \cref{sec:using-result}; a smaller initial step, or a
one-sided rule at an edge.


\paragraph{Aliasing.} A function that oscillates on a scale far below $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$ can lie about its slope
at every sample: $\sin(64 \pi x)$ at $x = 0$, whose first levels sample only zeros (\cref{sb:aliasing}), or
$\sin x$ at a large $\abs{x}$, where the default step spans many periods. \emph{Cure:} a step on the
oscillation's scale.

\paragraph{A noisy function.} A solver's output, or any function whose error changes irregularly from one argument
to the next, is accurate to $\noise$, not to $\epsmach$.
\emph{Symptom:} the error with the default step is far above $\epsmach^{2/3}$, and Ridders' estimate is optimistic.
\emph{Cure:} the step $\noise^{1/3} s$ (\cref{sec:using-solver}, \cref{sec:rounding-noise}).

\paragraph{A domain edge.} Ridders' method restarts below a step at which $f$ is not finite, but needs the nearest
bad point a minimum distance away. For $\log x$ with the default step for $f'$, a value is returned down to
$x = 2^{-\num{\valRiddersEdgeLimitExponent}}$, with an estimate grown to flag the loss; ten correct digits hold
only down to $x = 2^{-\num{\valRiddersEdgeTenDigitsExponent}}$ (measured, \cref{fig:ridders-edge}). Exactly at an
edge, use \code{Forward<P>} or \code{Backward<P>}. A fit evaluated outside its range: wrap it to return NaN there,
as \cref{sec:stencils-one-sided} does, so that a sample outside it fails loudly.

\paragraph{The fourth derivative in float.} Mostly rounding noise, \num{\valFloatFourthCentralRelError} for
\code{Central<2>} on $\mathrm{e}^x$ (median, \cref{fig:order-accuracy}); the compiler warns (\cref{sec:api-warning}).
\emph{Cure:} double.


\paragraph{One step for several orders.} The default step differs per method and order, from $\epsmach^{1/3} s$ for
$f'$ to $\epsmach^{1/6} s$ for $f''''$ with \code{Central<2>} (\cref{tab:default-steps}), since the rounding error
grows as $\epsmach / h^N$; a step tuned for $f'$ leaves nothing of $f''''$. \emph{Cure:} in generic code that
varies the order, pass no step.

\section{What the library does not do}\label{sec:using-not}

\code{nxx::deriv} differentiates a function, not a table. Tabulated data must be fitted or interpolated first, and the
derivative is then the fit's: a linear interpolant has a piecewise-constant derivative and a kink at every node, so
start from a smooth fit or a spline, and use the spline's own derivative when it offers one; numerically, keep the
stencil within one interval, or accept the error of a kink (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}).

\paragraph{Mixed partials and shared evaluations.} A mixed partial such as $\partial^2 A / \partial T \partial V$ is
two nested calls, the outer one in $V$ on a lambda that calls the inner one in $T$, at the product of their costs.
Let the lambda return the inner value, or a NaN when the inner call fails
(\code{d ? d->value : std::numeric_limits<double>::quiet_NaN()}), so that the outer call reports \code{non_finite}.
As a rule of thumb, the inner error, about $\epsmach^{2/3}$, is the noise level of the outer call: pass it the step
$\epsmach^{2/9} \max(\abs{V}, 1)$ and expect about $\epsmach^{4/9}$, roughly $10^{-7}$ in double. Cache an
expensive call's last argument and outputs so that its outputs share the evaluations.


\paragraph{Deferred features.} Gradients, Jacobians and Hessians (the loops of \cref{sec:using-sensitivity}
meanwhile); several outputs or orders from shared evaluations; a tolerance for Ridders' method; orders above four;
user stencils; a noise-aware step;\cite{more2012,gill1983} the complex step and automatic differentiation, which need
more of $f$ than a black box (\cref{sb:alternatives}); their status is in \cref{sec:guarantees-deferred}.
