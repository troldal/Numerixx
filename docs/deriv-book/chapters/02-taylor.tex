\chapter[Finite differences from Taylor's theorem]{Finite differences from\\ Taylor's theorem}\label{ch:taylor}

\Cref{fig:secant} showed that the secant through $x - h$ and $x + h$ lies closer to the tangent than the secant
through $x$ and $x + h$. This chapter says by how much, with one tool: Taylor's theorem. It gives the two simplest
finite differences, the error each makes, and a rule that predicts how that error changes with the step. All of it
can be done with pencil and paper, and we measure every prediction on the heat capacity of nitrogen.

\section{The secant and the tangent}\label{sec:taylor-secant}

The slope of the secant through $x$ and $x + h$ is the \emph{forward difference}
\begin{equation}
  f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}.
  \label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
How far is it from $f'(x)$? Taylor's theorem answers that. If $f$ has a continuous second derivative between $x$
and $x + h$, then
\begin{equation*}
  f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(\xi)
\end{equation*}
for some point $\xi$ between $x$ and $x + h$.\cite{burden2016} The first two terms are the tangent line; the last is
the \emph{remainder}, the price of stopping the series there. We do not know $\xi$, but we know that it lies in the
interval. Subtract $f(x)$ and divide by $h$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \frac{h}{2} f''(\xi).
  \label{eq:forward-error}
\end{equation}
The forward difference is $f'(x)$ plus an error of $h f''(\xi) / 2$. This error is the \emph{truncation error}: the
error of the formula itself, with every number computed exactly, so called because it comes from truncating the
Taylor series. The picture says the same thing: a secant differs from the tangent because the curve bends, and the
bending is $f''$.

For the enthalpy of nitrogen, $\enth'' = \dd\cp / \dd T$ is positive at $T_0$, where the closed form gives
\SI{\valCpSecondExact}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin\squared} (the heat capacity still rises at
\SI{1000}{\kelvin}), so the forward difference comes out too high, by about half of $\enth''$ times the step.

The library calls this rule \code{Forward<1>}.\mnote{\code{P} in \code{Forward<P>} and \code{Central<P>} is the
accuracy order, not the number of points: \code{Central<2>} uses two points for $f'$, \code{Forward<1>} two.} The
number in the name is its accuracy order, defined in \cref{sec:taylor-order}. A rule is written in the library, and
in this book, as a set of \emph{nodes} $n_k$, the offsets of the sample points in units of $h$, and their
\emph{weights} $w_k$: the estimate is $\sum_k w_k f(x + n_k h) / h$. \Cref{tab:two-point} gives the nodes and
weights of the two rules of this chapter.

\section{Centring cancels a term}\label{sec:taylor-central}

Now expand $f$ on both sides of $x$, one term further. With $f$ smooth enough,
\begin{align*}
  f(x + h) &= f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \frac{h^4}{24} f^{(4)}(x) + \cdots\\
  f(x - h) &= f(x) - h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \frac{h^4}{24} f^{(4)}(x) - \cdots
\end{align*}
The terms with even powers of $h$ are the same in both lines, and the terms with odd powers differ only in sign.
Subtracting the second line from the first therefore removes $f(x)$, $f''$ and $f^{(4)}$ and doubles the rest.
Divide by $2h$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + \frac{h^4}{120} f^{(5)}(x) + \cdots
  \label{eq:central}
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{central difference}, the library's \code{Central<2>} and its default rule. The term with $h$ has
gone: the bending $f''$, which spoils the forward difference, affects the two sides equally and cancels. What is
left starts with $h^2$ and involves $f'''$, the rate at which the bending changes. With the remainder in Lagrange's
form instead of the infinite series, the result reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(\xi), \qquad x - h < \xi < x + h,
  \label{eq:central-error}
\end{equation}
which holds whenever $f'''$ is continuous on the interval; the sidebar below proves it and shows why the next term is
of order $h^4$ (\cref{sb:remainder}).

The sign of the error is the sign of $f'''$: the central difference overestimates $f'$ where $f''' > 0$ and
underestimates it where $f''' < 0$. For nitrogen at \SI{1000}{\kelvin}, $\enth''' = \dd^2 \cp / \dd T^2$ is negative
(the heat capacity rises ever more slowly), so the central difference comes out slightly low.

Note what the central difference does not use: the value $f(x)$ has weight zero (\cref{tab:two-point}). It costs two
evaluations of $f$, the same as the forward difference, and is far more accurate for small $h$. That is why it is
the default.

There is a second way to see the cancellation. The mirror image of the forward difference is the \emph{backward
difference} $(f(x) - f(x - h)) / h$, the library's \code{Backward<1>}, the rule for a function known only below $x$.
By the same expansion its error is $-h f''(x) / 2 + \order(h^2)$,\mnote{$\order(h^2)$, ``of order $h^2$'': terms
of size $h^2$ and smaller, which shrink at least as fast as $h^2$ (\cref{sec:taylor-order}).} the same size as the forward difference's, with
the opposite sign. The central difference is exactly the mean of the two,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}
  = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} + \frac{f(x) - f(x - h)}{h} \right),
\end{equation*}
so their errors of order $h$ cancel, and what remains is of order $h^2$.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:remainder]{Where the constant 1/6 comes from}
Taylor's theorem with the remainder in Lagrange's form says: if $f$ has $n + 1$ continuous derivatives between $x$
and $x + h$, then
\begin{equation*}
  f(x + h) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \frac{h^j}{j!} f^{(j)}(x) + \frac{h^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} f^{(n+1)}(\xi)
\end{equation*}
for some $\xi$ strictly between $x$ and $x + h$; the same holds with $-h$ in place of $h$.\cite{burden2016} Take
$n = 2$ on both sides of $x$:
\begin{equation*}
  f(x \pm h) = f(x) \pm h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) \pm \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(\xi_\pm),
\end{equation*}
with $\xi_+$ in $(x, x + h)$ and $\xi_-$ in $(x - h, x)$. Subtract and divide by $2h$:
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6} \cdot \frac{f'''(\xi_+) + f'''(\xi_-)}{2}.
\end{equation*}
The mean of $f'''(\xi_+)$ and $f'''(\xi_-)$ lies between the two values, and a continuous function takes every value
between two of its values (the intermediate value theorem). So the mean equals $f'''(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ in
$(x - h, x + h)$, which is \cref{eq:central-error}. The $1/6$ is $1/3!$ from the cubic term, unchanged because the
two cubic terms add.

Why the next term is $h^4$: in the series for $f(x + h) - f(x - h)$ every even power of $h$ cancels, so only odd
powers $h, h^3, h^5, \ldots$ remain; after the division by $2h$ only even powers $h^0, h^2, h^4, \ldots$ are left.
The error of the central difference is a series in $h^2$. The forward difference has no such symmetry, and its error
contains every power of $h$.
\end{sidebar}

\section{Order of accuracy}\label{sec:taylor-order}

Both error terms have the form constant times a power of $h$ times a derivative of $f$: $\frac{1}{2} h^1 f''$ for
the forward difference and $\frac{1}{6} h^2 f'''$ for the central one. In general a rule for the $N$-th derivative
has a truncation error whose leading term is
\begin{equation*}
  \errc\, h^{\aorder} f^{(\aorder + \dorder)}(x),
\end{equation*}
and $\aorder$ is its \emph{accuracy order}.\mnote{The \emph{derivative order} $\dorder$ says which derivative is
computed; this chapter has $\dorder = 1$ throughout. \Cref{ch:orders} treats $\dorder = 2$ to 4.} The forward
difference has accuracy order 1 and the central difference accuracy order 2; we write that their errors are
$\order(h)$ and $\order(h^2)$. The statement is about small steps: as $h$ tends to 0, the error divided by
$h^{\aorder}$ stays bounded, and the leading term describes the error better and better. For larger steps the next
terms matter too; for the central difference the next one is of order $h^{\aorder + 2}$, here $h^4$, as
\cref{eq:central} shows.

The practical meaning is a prediction. While the leading term dominates, halving the step divides the error by
$2^{\aorder}$: it halves the error of the forward difference and quarters that of the central difference. To gain a
factor of 10 in accuracy, the forward difference needs a step 10 times smaller, the central difference a step only
$\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ times smaller.

Halving the step divides the forward error by a little more than 2 and the central error by exactly 4. The error
terms predict the sizes too: $h f''/2 = 3h$ gives $0.3$ and $0.15$ for the forward difference, the small rest being
the next term, $h^2$; and $h^2 f'''/6 = h^2$ gives $1/100$ and $1/400$ for the central difference, exactly, because
the fourth and higher derivatives of a cubic vanish.

How small must $h$ be for the leading term to dominate? Small compared with the distance over which $f$ changes its
character, so that the higher derivatives in the next terms do not outweigh the powers of $h$ they come with. For the
enthalpy of nitrogen, a smooth function of $T/\SI{1000}{\kelvin}$, steps of many kelvin are already small in this
sense. Most engineering functions vary on a scale comparable to the size of their argument; \cref{ch:rounding}
builds the library's default step on that observation, and shows what to do when it fails.

\Cref{fig:fd-slopes} tests the prediction on the thread, $\enth(T)$ at $T_0 = \SI{1000}{\kelvin}$, with the closed
form of $\cp$ as the answer key. On logarithmic axes an error $\errc h^{\aorder}$ is a straight line of slope
$\aorder$, and the two measured curves are such lines, of slopes 1 and 2. At every step shown, the central difference
is more accurate by orders of magnitude.

\section{Exactness on polynomials}\label{sec:taylor-exact}

The error terms give a second, exact check that needs no computer. The forward difference has the error
$h f''(\xi) / 2$, which vanishes when $f'' = 0$: the forward difference is exact for every straight line, as it
must be, since a secant of a line is the line. The central difference has the error $h^2 f'''(\xi) / 6$, which
vanishes when $f''' = 0$: it is exact for every quadratic. With pencil and paper, for $f(x) = a + b x + c x^2$,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} = \frac{2bh + 4cxh}{2h} = b + 2cx = f'(x)
\end{equation*}
for every $h$. For a cubic it is not exact: $f(x) = x^3$ gives $3x^2 + h^2$, and the excess $h^2$ is the error term
$h^2 f''' / 6$ with $f''' = 6$. In general a rule of accuracy order $\aorder$ for the $\dorder$-th derivative is exact
for polynomials of degree up to $\aorder + \dorder - 1$ (\cref{ch:stencils}). ``Exact'' here means in exact
arithmetic; a computer still rounds the values, which is the subject of the next chapter.

The structure of \cref{eq:central} also shows where more accuracy can come from. Write $\Dc{h}$ for the central
difference at step $h$. Its error is $\frac{1}{6} h^2 f''' + \order(h^4)$, so the error of $\Dc{h/2}$ is a quarter
of that, and the combination
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{4 \Dc{h/2} - \Dc{h}}{3} = f'(x) + \order(h^4)
\end{equation*}
cancels the $h^2$ term. This is the four-point rule \code{Central<4>} of \cref{ch:stencils} in disguise
(\cref{sb:richardson-disguise}), and repeated, the idea of Ridders' method (\cref{ch:ridders}).

\section{What this chapter did not say}\label{sec:taylor-not-said}

Three assumptions stand behind every formula of this chapter.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Exact arithmetic.} The values of $f$ are rounded, and \cref{fig:fd-slopes} stops at
    \SI{0.01}{\kelvin} because below that step the central difference stops improving. Why, and how to choose the
    step, is \cref{ch:rounding}.
  \item \emph{Two points.} More points buy a higher accuracy order, and one-sided rules of higher order exist
    (\cref{ch:stencils}); higher derivatives follow in \cref{ch:orders}.
  \item \emph{Smoothness.} The error terms need $f''$ or $f'''$ to exist and be continuous on the whole interval
    the rule samples. Where $f$ has a kink or a jump inside that interval, the error terms say nothing, and the
    result can be wrong without any warning (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}).
\end{itemize}
