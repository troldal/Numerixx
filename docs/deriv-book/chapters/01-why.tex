\chapter{Why numerical derivatives}\label{ch:why}

Engineering models are full of derivatives that nobody writes down. A heat capacity is the slope of an enthalpy, a
Jacobian is the slope of a set of balances, and a sensitivity is the slope of a result with respect to a parameter.
When the function is a formula on paper, the derivative is a matter of calculus. When the function is a program, it
often is not. This chapter says where such derivatives arise, which tools exist for them, and what the library
\nxx{} offers; it ends with a first computation and a half-page summary for the reader in a hurry.

\section{Derivatives without a formula}\label{sec:why-without-formula}

A function in engineering software is usually a piece of code that takes a number and returns a number. Typical
cases are these.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Property models.} A heat capacity $\cp = \dd \enth / \dd T$ from an enthalpy $\enth(T)$, a
    compressibility from an equation of state, a vapour-pressure slope for the Clausius--Clapeyron equation. The
    model is a correlation inside a property library, often in several pieces.
  \item \emph{Simulators.} The outlet temperature of a heat exchanger as a function of a feed flow, computed by a
    simulation that iterates to convergence.
  \item \emph{Implicit functions.} A friction factor defined by an equation it must satisfy, or a flash temperature
    defined by an energy balance: the value comes from a solver, and no formula for it exists.
  \item \emph{Sensitivities.} How much a reaction rate changes per kelvin, or per unit of activation energy: the
    numbers a design review asks for.
  \item \emph{Newton's method.} Solving $g(T) = 0$ needs the slope $g'(T)$ at every iteration.
\end{itemize}
In every case the function can be \emph{evaluated} at any point we like, but its derivative is not given. Deriving
it by hand from the code is tedious and error-prone, and impossible when the code is not ours.

The way out is old and simple. The derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of the tangent to the curve of $f$ at $x$, and
the slope of a line through two nearby points of the curve, a \emph{secant}, is close to it
(\cref{fig:secant}).\mnote{The \emph{step} $h$ is the distance between $x$ and the points at which $f$ is
evaluated.} Evaluate $f$ at $x$ and at $x + h$, divide the change of $f$ by $h$, and we have an estimate of $f'(x)$:
a \emph{finite difference}. The secant through $x - h$ and $x + h$, placed symmetrically around $x$, is much closer
to the tangent than the one through $x$ and $x + h$, as the figure shows. \Cref{ch:taylor} explains why, and
\cref{ch:rounding} why the step cannot simply be made as small as we like.

\section{The alternatives in one page}\label{sec:why-alternatives}

A finite difference is not the only way to a derivative, and not always the best.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The analytic derivative}, by hand or by computer algebra, is exact and cheap to evaluate. Use it when
    the formula is at hand and short enough to differentiate without mistakes.
  \item \emph{Automatic differentiation} runs the program with a special number type that carries a derivative
    along with every value, and is exact to rounding. It needs the source code, written for a generic number type.
  \item \emph{The complex step} evaluates $f$ once at a complex argument and reads $f'$ from the imaginary part of
    the result, again exact to rounding. It needs an $f$ that accepts complex numbers and is analytic.
  \item \emph{A finite difference} needs only values of $f$ at real points. It works on any function the program
    can evaluate: a library call, a solver, legacy code. Its price is accuracy (about two thirds of the digits of
    the number type, \cref{ch:rounding}) and two or more evaluations of $f$.
\end{itemize}
This book is about the last: the tool for the function that is a black box. When the first three are open to you,
they are better, and the sidebar below says when (\cref{sb:alternatives}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:alternatives]{When automatic differentiation or the complex step is the better tool}
\emph{Forward-mode automatic differentiation} replaces each number $v$ in the computation by a pair $(v, v')$ and
each operation by its rule of calculus: $(u, u') \cdot (v, v') = (uv, u'v + uv')$, and so on. Seeding $x$ with
$(x, 1)$ gives $(f(x), f'(x))$ at the end, exact to rounding, at a cost of a small multiple of one evaluation. It
needs the function's source, written for a generic number type (a C++ template, for instance), and a library that
supplies the pair type.\cite{griewank2008}

The \emph{complex step} uses $f'(x) \approx \operatorname{Im} f(x + \mathrm{i} h) / h$. No two nearly equal values
are subtracted, so the step can be taken far below the limit that rounding sets for a finite difference, and the
result is exact to rounding with one evaluation. It needs $f$ implemented for complex arguments and analytic near
$x$: an absolute value, a comparison or a table lookup inside $f$ breaks it. It is a form of automatic
differentiation in disguise.\cite{squire1998} Lyness and Moler's contour-integral methods extend the idea to higher
derivatives of analytic functions.\cite{lyness1967}

\code{nxx::deriv} takes any callable \code{fn} that maps a real number to a real number, and assumes nothing else:
not its source, not a complex extension, not even smoothness beyond what the error terms of the later chapters
need. That is the case the other tools do not cover. Automatic differentiation and the complex step are deferred
features of the library, named as such in \cref{ch:using}.
\end{sidebar}

\section{What the library offers}\label{sec:why-offers}

\code{nxx::deriv} is the numerical-derivatives module of \nxx, a header-only C++23 library. Everything goes
through one function, \code{derivative}, with these choices.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Four methods.} \code{Central<P>}, the default, samples $f$ on both sides of $x$; \code{Forward<P>}
    and \code{Backward<P>} sample on one side only, for a function defined on one side; \code{Ridders} adapts its
    step and also estimates its own error. The first three are \emph{stencils}:\mnote{A \emph{stencil} samples $f$
    at a fixed pattern of points around $x$ and combines the samples with fixed weights. Ridders' method decides
    its points as it goes.} \code{P} is the accuracy order of \cref{ch:taylor}.
  \item \emph{Derivatives of order 1 to 4}, $f'$ to $f^{(4)}$.
  \item \emph{Any real type}: \code{float}, \code{double}, or a user type with more digits such as those of
    Boost.Multiprecision.
  \item \emph{No exceptions.} A failure is a value: the result is a \code{std::expected} that holds either an
    estimate or the error \code{non_finite}.
  \item \emph{A default step}, so that a call needs only the function and the point.
\end{itemize}
The calls themselves are the subject of \cref{ch:using}; \cref{ch:api} lists every one.

\section{A first example}\label{sec:why-first}

The example that runs through Part~I is the heat capacity of a gas from its enthalpy. For nitrogen the NIST
Chemistry WebBook gives the molar enthalpy in the Shomate form,\mnote{The \emph{Shomate form}: a fitted polynomial
in $T/1000$ plus a $1/T$ term, one set of coefficients per temperature range.} a fit to the NIST-JANAF thermochemical
tables.\cite{nist-webbook,chase1998} We use the range \SIrange{500}{2000}{\kelvin} and the point
$T_0 = \SI{1000}{\kelvin}$. The function returns $\enth(T)$, the enthalpy relative to its value at
\SI{298.15}{\kelvin}, in \si{\joule\per\mole}, so that $\dd \enth / \dd T$ is $\cp$ in
\si{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} directly. The coefficients and the function are defined once, in a header shared by
every example of the book:
\snippetheader{shomate}{shomate}
\needspace{14\baselineskip}%
\snippetheader{shomate}{shomate-H}

The heat capacity is one call. Without a method or a step, \code{derivative} uses \code{Central<2>}, the central
difference of \cref{fig:secant}, with its default step:
\snippet{01-why-first}{first}
It prints $\cp = \num{\valCpDefaultValueLong}$\,\si{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}. The default step here is
\SI{\valCpDefaultStep}{\kelvin}; \cref{ch:rounding} explains where it comes from.

How good is that? For this function we know the answer. The WebBook also publishes the fit's $\cp(T)$, the exact
derivative of $\enth(T)$, which gives $\cp(T_0) = \num{\valCpExactLong}$\,\si{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}. The
estimate is off by \num{\valCpDefaultRelError} relative. This closed form is the \emph{answer key} of the book. We differentiate a function whose derivative we know
so that every error can be measured; in practice you would differentiate one whose derivative you do not know, and
\cref{ch:using} does so with a friction factor that comes from a solver. The closed form is exact for the
\emph{function}, not for nitrogen: the fit itself reproduces the tabulated heat capacity of nitrogen to about four
significant figures, which is a matter for the property data, not for the derivative.

Ridders' method adapts its step and reports an estimate of its own error in \code{abs_error}:
\snippet{01-why-first}{first-ridders}
After \num{\valCpRiddersEvals} evaluations of $\enth$, against the stencil's two, it returns
$\cp = \num{\valCpRiddersValue}$\,\si{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} with an error estimate of
\SI{\valCpRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}. The actual error is
\SI{\valCpRiddersActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}, or \num{\valCpRiddersRelError} relative, so the estimate is
honest here: it is larger than the actual error. \Cref{ch:ridders} shows how the
method works and how far its estimate can be trusted.

Both listings test the result before using it: \code{if (d)} is true when \code{derivative} returned an estimate,
and only then is \code{d->value} read. The test is not a formality. A call fails, with the error \code{error::non_finite},
for example when \code{fn} returns an infinite or NaN value at a point the method samples, or when the step is too small to move
$x$ (\cref{ch:rounding}).

\paragraph{Accuracy.} At the default step a first derivative in \code{double} has about
