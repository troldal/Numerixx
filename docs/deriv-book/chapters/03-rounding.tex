\chapter{Rounding and the choice of step}\label{ch:rounding}

\Cref{ch:taylor} predicts that a smaller step gives a smaller error: halve $h$, and the error of the central
difference falls by four. A computer does not follow the prediction for long. Pass the step yourself, as the third
argument of \code{derivative}, and compare a moderate step with a tiny one:
\snippet{03-rounding-step}{explicit-step}
The step of \SI{e-8}{\kelvin} is worse than the step of \SI{e-2}{\kelvin}, by orders of magnitude. This chapter
explains why, finds the best step, and shows how the library's default step is built, when it is wrong, and what to
do then.

\section{Floating-point numbers in brief}\label{sec:rounding-fp}

A \code{double} stores a number with \num{\valDigitsDouble} binary digits, about 16 significant decimal digits. Numbers
are therefore spaced: just above 1 the next \code{double} is $1 + \epsmach$, where
\begin{equation*}
  \epsmach = 2^{-52} \approx \num{\valEpsDouble}
\end{equation*}
is the \emph{machine epsilon} of the type.\mnote{\code{std::numeric_limits<T>::epsilon()} in C++.} The spacing grows
in proportion to the numbers themselves: near \num{1000} the doubles are \num{\valUlpAtOneThousand} apart. Every
arithmetic operation rounds its exact result to the nearest representable number, which changes it by at most half a
spacing, a relative error of at most $\epsmach / 2$.\cite{higham2002,goldberg1991} A value of $f$ computed by a few
dozen operations is therefore wrong by a few units of $\epsmach$ relative, sometimes less, sometimes more. We model
that with one number: each value of $f$ carries an error of about $\epsmach \abs{f}$. Nothing more about floating
point is needed to follow this chapter; \cref{ch:floating-point} has the details, and \cref{sb:ulp} in
\cref{sec:rounding-default} what the spacing of the numbers near $x$ means for a step.

\section{The two errors and the V}\label{sec:rounding-v}

\Cref{fig:v-curve} shows the error of the central difference for the heat capacity of nitrogen over fifteen decades
of step. From the right, the error falls along a line of slope 2, as \cref{ch:taylor} predicts, until about
\SI{0.01}{\kelvin}; from there to the left it \emph{rises} again, along a line of slope $-1$, and at the smallest
steps the result has no correct digit at all. The curve has the shape of a V.

$E(h) / \cp$ of \cref{eq:error-model} dashed. Right of the bottom truncation dominates and the error falls as $h^2$;
left of it rounding dominates and the error grows as $1/h$. The default step sits near the bottom.\label{fig:v-curve}}
\centering
\input{figures/03-rounding-v-curve}
\end{figure}

The right branch is the truncation error of \cref{eq:central-error}. The left branch is rounding. The central
difference subtracts $f(x - h)$ from $f(x + h)$, two numbers that agree in more and more of their leading digits as
$h$ shrinks. The subtraction itself is exact, but it removes the digits the two values share and leaves only those in
which they differ, and those include the rounding errors of both values. The rounding errors were tiny compared with
$f$; they are not tiny compared with the difference. This loss of digits is called \emph{cancellation}.

A pocket calculator that keeps six significant digits shows the whole V in four lines. Differentiate $\mathrm{e}^x$ at
$x = 1$, where $f'(1) = \mathrm{e} = \num{\valHandCalcExact}\ldots$, rounding each value of $f$ to six digits:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
\toprule
$h$ & $f(1 + h)$ & $f(1 - h)$ & estimate & error\\
\midrule
\tablebody{hand-calculator}
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
From $h = 0.1$ to $0.01$ the error falls by a factor of only \num{\valHandCalcErrorRatio}, not the 100 that
truncation alone would give: at $h = 0.01$ the truncation error is about \num{\valHandCalcTruncationHundredth}, and
the rest of the error in that row is already rounding. Below that step the error rises. At $h = 10^{-4}$ the two
values share their first four digits; their difference, \num{\valHandCalcLastDiff}, has only two digits left, and
the last of them is rounding. A \code{double} keeps about 16 digits instead of six, so the same happens at smaller
steps, but it happens.

Both errors can be written down. If each value of $f$ is off by up to about $\epsmach \abs{f}$, the difference
$f(x + h) - f(x - h)$ is off by up to about $2 \epsmach \abs{f}$, and after the division by $2h$ the estimate of $f'$
by up to about $\epsmach \abs{f} / h$. Adding the truncation error of \cref{eq:central-error} gives the \emph{error
model} of the central difference,
\begin{equation}
  E(h) = \etrunc(h) + \eround(h) = \frac{h^2}{6} \abs{f'''(x)} + \frac{\epsmach \abs{f(x)}}{h}.
  \label{eq:error-model}
\end{equation}
The first term falls with $h$ and the second grows; their sum is the V. The dashed line in \cref{fig:v-curve} is this
model for the nitrogen example, and the measured curve follows it, with one difference: on the rounding branch the measured
errors scatter below the model. The rounding errors of the two values have random signs and partly cancel, so the
model gives their typical largest size, not their actual size at a particular step.

The model gives an \emph{absolute} error, in the unit of $f'$; the figure divides it by $\cp$. It leaves out a second
rounding, of the points $x \pm h$ themselves, which the library removes for the central difference by dividing by
the distance actually evaluated (\cref{sb:actual-distance}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:actual-distance]{Dividing by the distance actually evaluated}
The points $x + h$ and $x - h$ are computed, so they are rounded: the function is evaluated at
$x_+ = \fl(x + h)$ and $x_- = \fl(x - h)$, each within half a spacing of the intended point, so their distance can
differ from $2h$ by up to about $\epsmach \abs{x}$. Dividing by the nominal $2h$ would add a relative error of about
$\epsmach \abs{x} / (2h)$. At the default step $h = \epsmach^{1/3} \abs{x}$ that is $\epsmach^{2/3} / 2$: as large as
the accuracy aimed for.

The two-node rules (\code{Central<2>}, \code{Forward<1>} and \code{Backward<1>} for $f'$) therefore divide by
$x_+ - x_-$ as evaluated. The quotient is then the exact slope of the secant through the two points where $f$ was
actually evaluated, and only the rounding of the values of $f$ remains. The subtraction $x_+ - x_-$ is itself exact
when $x_+ \le 2 x_-$ (for positive $x$), by Sterbenz's lemma (Theorem 4.3.1):\cite{sterbenz1974} two floating-point
numbers of the same sign within a factor of two of each other have an exactly representable
difference (Higham's Theorem 2.5).\cite{higham2002} That holds roughly for
$h \le \abs{x} / 3$. For larger steps the subtraction rounds once, a relative error of at most $\epsmach / 2$, which
is harmless.

Rules with more nodes divide by a fixed multiple of the nominal step instead. For them the actual distance would not
help: for \code{Central<4>}, dividing by the distance between the outer nodes would quadruple their point rounding
(its coefficient rises from $1/12$ to $4/12$) and leave the inner nodes' untouched. They keep a rounding term from the
points, about
$(\ampl / 2)\, \epsmach \abs{x f'} / h$, with $\ampl$ the sum of the absolute weights (\cref{ch:stencils}).
\end{sidebar}

\section{The best step and the best accuracy}\label{sec:rounding-best}

The bottom of the V is where the model is smallest. Setting the derivative of \cref{eq:error-model} with respect to
$h$ to zero, $h \abs{f'''} / 3 - \epsmach \abs{f} / h^2 = 0$, gives the \emph{best step}
\begin{equation}
  \hbest = \left( \frac{3 \epsmach \abs{f}}{\abs{f'''}} \right)^{1/3},
  \qquad
  E(\hbest) = \frac{3}{2} \frac{\epsmach \abs{f}}{\hbest}.
  \label{eq:best-step}
\end{equation}
Both depend on $f$ and $f'''$, which we do not know; their dependence on $\epsmach$ is what matters. Suppose $f$
varies on a length scale $w$, in the units of $x$, so that each derivative is about the previous one divided by $w$:
$\abs{f'} \approx \abs{f} / w$ and $\abs{f'''} \approx \abs{f} / w^3$.\mnote{The \emph{scale} $w$ of $f$: the
distance in $x$ over which $f$ changes appreciably.} Then
\begin{equation*}
  \hbest \approx 3^{1/3} \epsmach^{1/3} w,
  \qquad
  \frac{E(\hbest)}{\abs{f'}} \approx \epsmach^{2/3}.
\end{equation*}
For a \code{double}, $\epsmach^{1/3} = \num{\valRootEpsThreeDouble}$ and $\epsmach^{2/3} = \num{\valModelCentralTwoFirstDouble}$:
at its best step the central difference keeps about \num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoFirstDouble} of the 16
digits (Nocedal and Wright,\cite{nocedal2006} Section 8.1). The same argument for the forward difference, with its error $h \abs{f''} / 2$, gives a best
step near $\epsmach^{1/2} w$ and a relative error near $\epsmach^{1/2} = \num{\valModelForwardOneFirstDouble}$, about
8 digits. That difference, not the cost, is why the central difference is the default.

The relative figure $\epsmach^{2/3}$ rests on the assumption $\abs{f} \approx w \abs{f'}$. The rounding term of
\cref{eq:error-model} grows with $\abs{f}$, not with $\abs{f'}$, so a function whose values are large compared with
$w \abs{f'}$ loses accuracy in proportion: about $\log_{10} (\abs{f} / (w \abs{f'}))$ digits. For the nitrogen example, with
$w = T_0$, $\abs{\enth} / (T_0 \abs{\cp}) = \num{\valCpRelativeFactor}$, close to 1. Had the enthalpy been referred to
\SI{0}{\kelvin}, or included an enthalpy of formation, $\abs{\enth}$ would be larger and the derivative proportionally
less accurate, although $\cp$ would be the same: a formation enthalpy of a few hundred kilojoules per mole, against
$T_0 \cp$ of a few tens, costs about one digit. Near a point where $f'$ vanishes, the relative error has no meaning at
all, while the absolute error stays near $\epsmach^{2/3} \abs{f} / w$.

On the nitrogen example the measured curve reaches its lowest point, \num{\valCpBestRelError}, at \SI{\valCpBestStep}{\kelvin}.
That is below the model, because the rounding errors happen to cancel there; a neighbouring step does several times
worse. The model's level, not the luckiest point, is what to expect.

\section{The default step}\label{sec:rounding-default}

The library does not know $f$, so it can compute neither $\hbest$ nor the scale $w$. It takes the formula of
\cref{sec:rounding-best} without its constant, and guesses the scale:
\begin{equation}
  h = \epsmach^{1/3} \scale, \qquad \scale = \max(\abs{x}, 1).
  \label{eq:default-step}
\end{equation}
The scale $\scale$ is the library's guess for $w$. For large $\abs{x}$ it makes the step relative, a fixed fraction
of $x$: functions of a physical quantity often vary on a scale comparable to its size, as the enthalpy varies with
$T/\SI{1000}{\kelvin}$. Near zero $\abs{x}$ says nothing about the scale, and the step becomes absolute,
$\epsmach^{1/3}$ in the units of $x$. This choice of scale is
standard in numerical optimisation (Dennis and Schnabel,\cite{dennis1996} Section 5.4). For the nitrogen example, $\scale = \SI{1000}{\kelvin}$ and the default
step is \SI{\valCpDefaultStep}{\kelvin}; the relative error is \num{\valCpDefaultRelError}, on the model line near the
bottom of the V (\cref{fig:v-curve}). Every stencil of the library has a default step of the same form,
$\epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} \scale$ (\cref{ch:stencils,ch:orders}).

Dropping the constant $3^{1/3} \approx \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFirst}$ makes the default step smaller than
the model's best step, by that factor when the guess is right, $w = \scale$. It costs little, and the guess itself is
the weaker link (\cref{sb:step-constant}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:step-constant]{The error model behind the default step, and why its constant is dropped}
For a rule of accuracy order $\aorder$ for the $\dorder$-th derivative, the error model is
$E(h) = \errc h^{\aorder} \abs{f^{(\aorder + \dorder)}} + \ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / h^{\dorder}$, with the error
constant $\errc$ and the sum of absolute weights $\ampl$ of the rule (\cref{ch:stencils}); for the central difference
$\errc = 1/6$, $\ampl = 1$ and $\dorder = 1$, which is \cref{eq:error-model}. It is smallest at
\begin{equation*}
  \hbest = \left( \frac{\dorder \ampl \epsmach \abs{f}}{\aorder \errc \abs{f^{(\aorder + \dorder)}}}
  \right)^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)},
\end{equation*}
and if $f$ varies on the scale $w$, this is $k \epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} w$ with the constant
$k = (\dorder \ampl / (\aorder \errc))^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)}$. The default step leaves $k$ out and puts the guess
$\scale$ for $w$, so when the guess is right it equals $\hbest / k$. With $h = \hbest / k$ the model error grows by the factor
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{E(\hbest / k)}{E(\hbest)} = \frac{\dorder k^{-\aorder} + \aorder k^{\dorder}}{\aorder + \dorder}.
\end{equation*}
For the central difference, $k = 3^{1/3} \approx \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFirst}$, and the factor is
$7 \cdot 3^{1/3} / 9 \approx \num{\valStepCostCentralTwoFirst}$: the default step loses about a tenth of the best
accuracy. For the other first-derivative rules $k$ lies between that and 2 (\code{Forward<1>}), and the cost reaches
\num{\valStepCostCentralTenFirst} for \code{Central<10>}. For higher derivatives $k$ is larger
(\num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoSecond} for \code{Central<2>} and $f''$, \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFourth} for
$f^{(4)}$) and so is the cost (\cref{ch:orders}, \cref{sec:derivations-model}).

Why leave $k$ out at all? Because it only matters when the guess $w = \scale$ holds, and the guess is the weaker
link: a step off by a factor 2 either way costs up to a factor \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFirst} for the
central difference, more than leaving out $k$ costs, and a function that varies faster than $\scale$ wants a smaller step anyway.
On the nine test functions of the library's design record (\code{docs/deriv/PHASE4_API.md}), which vary faster than
$\max(\abs{x}, 1)$, half the default step did better for every order, $f'$ included. So the library keeps one
convention, $\epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} \scale$, for every rule and order, and leaves the scale to the caller
(\cref{sec:rounding-scale}). The derivation in full is in \cref{ch:derivations}.
\end{sidebar}

A step must also be large enough to move $x$. The doubles near \SI{1000}{\kelvin} are
\SI{\valUlpAtOneThousand}{\kelvin} apart, so $T_0 + \SI{e-14}{\kelvin}$ rounds back to $T_0$, and the two points of
the central difference would coincide. The library checks for coinciding points and returns
\code{error::non_finite} rather than divide by zero:
\needspace{7\baselineskip}
\snippet{03-rounding-step}{too-small}
The default step is far from this limit, since $\epsmach^{1/3} \scale$ is many spacings of $x$; a step you pass
yourself may not be. The resolution of $x$ and the rounding of $x + h$ are the subject of the sidebar below
(\cref{sb:ulp}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:ulp]{Machine epsilon, ulps and the rounding of $x + h$}
The spacing of the numbers at $x$ is its \emph{unit in the last place}, $\ulp(x) = \epsmach \cdot 2^{e}$ for
$2^{e} \le \abs{x} < 2^{e+1}$: $\ulp(1) = \epsmach$, just below 1 the spacing is half of that, and
$\ulp(1000) = \num{\valUlpAtOneThousand}$. Rounding to nearest errs by at most $\ulp(x) / 2$, the \emph{unit
roundoff} $\epsmach / 2$ relative; this book and the library's rounding model use $\epsmach$, a factor 2 of safety
(\cref{sec:fp-epsilon}).

Since $x + h$ is rounded, the point the function sees is $\fl(x + h)$, not $x + h$. If $h$ is below half a spacing of
$x$, $\fl(x + h) = x$: the step has no effect. When this happens on both sides, both points round back to $x$, no
difference can be formed, and the library reports \code{error::non_finite}. Near a power of two, however, the spacing
differs on the two sides: below $2^e$ the numbers are twice as dense as above it, so a step can move $x$ downwards
and not upwards. The central difference then divides by the half-spacing that remains and returns a finite value
that is mostly rounding, without failing. The rules with more than two nodes check both $x + h$ and $x - h$ and fail
instead (\cref{sec:api-derivative}). Hence the documented requirement: a step well above $\ulp(x)$.
\end{sidebar}

\section{When the default step is wrong: the scale of $f$}\label{sec:rounding-scale}

The default step assumes that the guess is right, $w = \scale$: that $f$ varies on the scale $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$, in
the units of $x$. Two common situations break that assumption, and the remedy is the same for both.

\paragraph{A narrow feature at a large $\abs{x}$.} Take a function that changes from $-1$ to $1$ over a width of
about $w = 0.5$ around $x_0 = 1000$, such as $\tanh((x - x_0) / w)$, and differentiate it at $x = x_0 + 0.2$, as for a
smoothed switch or a sharp transition in a variable far larger than its width. Here $\scale = \num{1000.2}$, so the default step is \num{\valScaleDefaultStep}. That is small
against the width, but not small enough: the truncation error grows as $(h/w)^2$, not as $(h/\scale)^2$, and the
relative error is \num{\valScaleDefaultRelError}. \Cref{fig:step-scale} shows why: the default step sits far right of
the V's bottom. The remedy is to put the true scale $w$ in place of the guess $\scale$:
\begin{equation*}
  h = \epsmach^{1/3} w,
\end{equation*}
which gives the step \num{\valScaleFitStep} and the error \num{\valScaleFitRelError}, at the bottom of the V. Ridders'
method with an initial step on the same scale, $w/4$, is just as good: it returns an error of
\num{\valScaleRiddersUserRelError} with an estimate that covers it (\cref{ch:ridders}). In code, $\epsmach^{1/3}$ is
\code{root_epsilon<3, double>()}:\mnote{\code{nxx::math::root_epsilon<N, T>()} returns $\epsmach^{1/N}$ for the type
\code{T}, correct to within an ulp (\cref{sec:api-real}).}
\snippet{03-rounding-scale}{narrow-feature}

\paragraph{A small $\abs{x}$ in your units.} A mole fraction of $10^{-6}$, a strain of $10^{-4}$ or a time constant in
seconds of a fast process all have $\scale = 1$, so the default step is $\epsmach^{1/3}$, as large as the variable
itself or larger. Take $y \ln y$, which appears in every entropy of mixing, at $y = 10^{-6}$. The default step is
\num{\valSmallDefaultStep}, which exceeds $y$; the central difference samples $y - h < 0$, where the logarithm is not
defined, and the call fails with \code{error::non_finite}. With the step $\epsmach^{1/3} y = \num{\valSmallFitStep}$
the relative error is \num{\valSmallFitRelError}, as accurate as at any ordinary point (\cref{fig:step-scale-small}).
The 1 in $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$ is in your units.
\snippet{03-rounding-scale}{small-x}
Here the failure is loud, because the logarithm returns NaN below zero. A function that is defined across the bound
but meaningless there, such as a fit sampled outside its range, would return a wrong value without complaint; the
one-sided rules of \cref{ch:stencils} are the tool for such an edge.

Units matter at large $\abs{x}$ too: a property near room temperature varies on the scale of the absolute
temperature, but at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} the guess is $\scale = 20$ in degrees Celsius against 293 in kelvin, a
default step about 15 times too small, which on the rounding branch costs about one digit.

\paragraph{Finding the scale.} The scale $w$ can usually be read off the model. For a transition it is the width of
the transition; for a singularity or a bound at $a$, such as in $1/(x - a)$ or $\ln(x - a)$, the distance
$\abs{x - a}$; for an Arrhenius factor $\exp(-E_a / (\Rgas T))$, $\Rgas T^2 / E_a$, much smaller than $T$ for a large
activation energy; for a smooth interpolant of a table, such as a cubic spline, the node spacing, although a spline
is better differentiated by its own derivative (\cref{sec:using-not}). A rough value is enough: at the model's best
step, a step off by a factor of two either way costs \code{Central<2>} at most a factor
\num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFirst} in error. If the scale is hard to judge, Ridders' method with an initial step a
few times smaller than it chooses its own step and reports an error estimate (\cref{ch:ridders}).

\section{When $f$ is less accurate than $\epsmach$}\label{sec:rounding-noise}

The error model assumes that each value of $f$ is accurate to about $\epsmach$. Many engineering functions are not.
The output of an iterative solver stopped at a relative tolerance of $10^{-8}$, a flash calculation, or a simulation
that iterates to convergence is accurate only to that tolerance, and its error changes irregularly from one argument
to the next: it is \emph{noise}. Call its relative size the \emph{noise level} $\noise$. It takes the place of
$\epsmach$ in the rounding term of \cref{eq:error-model},
\begin{equation*}
  E(h) = \frac{h^2}{6} \abs{f'''} + \frac{\noise \abs{f}}{h},
\end{equation*}
and the argument of \cref{sec:rounding-best} carries over with $\noise$ for $\epsmach$: the best step is about
$\noise^{1/3} w$ and the best relative accuracy about $\noise^{2/3}$.\cite{more2012} A solver converged to
$10^{-8}$ gives about five correct digits, not \num{\valHeadlineFirstDigits}, and only at a step
$(\noise / \epsmach)^{1/3}$ times the default.

Two cautions on $\noise$. A solver's tolerance is the noise level only when its convergence test is on the output
itself, relative to it; a flash that converges a residual (an energy balance, equal fugacities) in its own units, or
a simulator that converges tear streams, has a tolerance that is not $\noise$: measure $\noise$, as shown below. And
if $f$ is computed by cancellation, as a difference of large terms, its rounding error is $\epsmach$ times the
largest intermediate, $k \epsmach \abs{f}$ with $k \gg 1$; treat $k \epsmach$ as the noise level.

A smooth but inaccurate $f$ is not noisy: the nitrogen Shomate fit describes nitrogen to about four digits, yet
its derivative, the fit's, is accurate to $\epsmach^{2/3}$.

\centering

\Cref{fig:noise} measures this on $\mathrm{e}^x$ at $x = 1$, whose values are multiplied by $1 + \noise u$ with $u$
drawn uniformly from $[-1, 1]$, the same $u$ for the same $x$, as a converged solver would give. For
$\noise = 10^{-8}$ the default step returns an error of \num{\valNoiseEightDefaultRelError}; the step
$\noise^{1/3} = \num{\valNoiseEightFitStep}$ gives \num{\valNoiseEightFitRelError}, close to the model's
$\noise^{2/3} = \num{\valNoiseEightModelBestRelError}$ (\code{code/03-rounding-noise.cpp}). So pass the step when $f$
is noisy, $h = \noise^{1/3} \scale$, with $\noise$ the solver's tolerance or your best estimate of the accuracy of
$f$: in code, \code{derivative(f, x, std::cbrt(eta) * std::max(std::abs(x), 1.0))}.

If the noise level is not known, it can be measured. Evaluate $f$ at a handful of points spaced far more closely
than the scale on which $f$ varies, and form their successive differences, as in a difference table. The $k$-th
differences of the smooth part of $f$ shrink like $h^k$, so at such a spacing the higher differences show the noise
alone, and their spread estimates $\noise \abs{f}$ up to a known factor: the $k$-th differences of independent noise
are $\sqrt{(2k)! / k!^2}$ times larger than the noise, and the recipe divides that out. It is a simplified form of
Mor\'e and Wild's procedure,\cite{more2011} kept in the book's example header:

$10^{-10}$, at eight points spaced by the default step,

it reports \num{\valNoiseEstimateFriction} relative to $f$ (order \num{\valNoiseEstimateFrictionK},
\num{\valNoiseEstimateEvals} evaluations); the actual root-mean-square deviation from the exact root is
\num{\valNoiseEstimateFrictionActual}. On the noisy $\mathrm{e}^x$ above it reports \num{\valNoiseEstimateNoisy}, near
$\noise / \sqrt{3}$ for $\noise = \num{\valNoiseEstimateNoisyEta}$; on the clean one, \num{\valNoiseEstimateClean}. If
no three orders agree, it reports $k = 0$: the spacing is too large for the smooth part to drop below the noise
within eight points; retry with a tenth of the spacing, or fall back to the solver's tolerance, as the listing does.
A spacing on the scale of $f$ itself can make the orders agree by accident at a wrong level (the full procedure tests
signs and adjusts the spacing): use the default step, check $k$, and distrust a level near $\epsmach$ for a solver.
The header, \code{docs/deriv-book/code/noise.hpp}, is under the repository's MIT licence.

Ridders' estimate assumes that $f$ is accurate to about $\epsmach$, so for a noisy $f$ it can be optimistic. Over \num{\valNoiseRiddersCases} points in $[0.2, 3]$ the
estimate covered the actual error in \SI{\valNoiseCleanRiddersCoverage}{\percent} of the cases for the clean
$\mathrm{e}^x$, but only in \SI{\valNoiseEightRiddersCoverage}{\percent} for $\noise = 10^{-8}$, where the worst
error was \num{\valNoiseEightRiddersWorstRatio} times its estimate. \Cref{sec:using-solver} differentiates the
friction factor with this rule.

\section{Float, double and more digits}\label{sec:rounding-types}

Everything above holds for any number type, with its own $\epsmach$. A \code{float}, $\epsmach =
\num{\valEpsFloat}$, reaches only about $\epsmach^{2/3} = \num{\valModelCentralTwoFirstFloat}$; on $\mathrm{e}^x$ at
1 its default step gives \num{\valFloatDefaultRelError} (\cref{fig:float-double}). A 50-digit type, $\epsmach =
\num{\valEpsMultiprecision}$, reaches far beyond \code{double} (\cref{sec:using-types}).

\section{A checklist for the step}\label{sec:rounding-checklist}

Before you accept the default step, answer six questions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{On what scale does $f$ vary, in the units of $x$?} If it is not about $\max(\abs{x}, 1)$, pass
    $h = \epsmach^{1/3} w$ for \code{Central<2>}, with $w$ that scale (\cref{sec:rounding-scale}).
  \item \emph{Is there a bound within a step of $x$}, such as zero for a mole fraction or the end of a fit's range?
    Use a smaller step, or a one-sided rule (\cref{ch:stencils}).
  \item \emph{How accurate is $f$?} If it is a solver output or otherwise noisy at a level $\noise$ above
    $\epsmach$, pass $h = \noise^{1/3} \scale$ and expect about $\noise^{2/3}$; if you do not know $\noise$, measure
    it (\cref{sec:rounding-noise}).
  \item \emph{Is $\abs{f}$ much larger than $\scale \abs{f'}$}, as for an enthalpy with a formation term or an
    absolute pressure with a small slope? Expect to lose about $\log_{10} (\abs{f} / (\scale \abs{f'}))$ digits;
    subtract a constant reference from $f$ before differentiating if you can (\cref{sec:rounding-best}).
  \item \emph{Which method and order?} A step chosen for one rule is wrong for another (\cref{ch:orders}).
  \item \emph{Unsure?} Use Ridders' method, which chooses its step and estimates its error (\cref{ch:ridders}), or
    repeat a stencil at $2h$ and $h/2$ (\cref{sec:using-check}).
\end{enumerate}
