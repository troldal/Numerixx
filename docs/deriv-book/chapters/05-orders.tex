\chapter{Second to fourth derivatives}\label{ch:orders}

The slope of a heat capacity, $\dd \cp / \dd T$, is the second derivative of the enthalpy; the curvature a solver
needs for a minimum is a second derivative too, and a stiffness or a Taylor coefficient may need the third or the
fourth. The library computes derivatives of order 1 to 4 with every method, through one more template argument.
This chapter shows the rules it uses, why each order costs digits and how many, what the default step does per
order, what the float warning means, and how Ridders' method extends to higher orders. The nitrogen example's second
derivative, $\enth''(T_0) = \dd \cp / \dd T$ at \SI{1000}{\kelvin}, runs through it.

\section{The rules for N = 2 to 4}\label{sec:orders-rules}

The derivative order is the second template argument of \code{derivative}, an integer or an enumerator of
\code{order}: \code{derivative<Central<2>, 2>(fn, x)} and \code{derivative<Central<2>, order::second>(fn, x)}
are the same call, and \code{derivative<M>} is \code{derivative<M, 1>}. The rules behind it come from the same
moment conditions as in \cref{sec:stencils-moments}, with $\mom{\dorder} = \dorder!$ in place of $\mom{1} = 1$.
For the second derivative on the nodes $-1, 0, 1$, a symmetric rule has the weights $a, b, a$; the conditions
$\mom{0} = 2a + b = 0$ and $\mom{2} = 2a = 2! = 2$ give $a = 1$ and $b = -2$, and $\mom{3} = 0$ by symmetry, so
\begin{equation}
  f''(x) = \frac{f(x - h) - 2 f(x) + f(x + h)}{h^2} - \frac{h^2}{12} f^{(4)}(\xi),
  \label{eq:orders-second}
\end{equation}
with the error from $\mom{4} = 2$ over $4!$. This is the three-point rule, the library's \code{Central<2>} for
$\dorder = 2$: the same tag, the same meaning. \code{Central<P>} is the symmetric rule whose error is
$\order(h^{\aorder})$ for every derivative order, and for the $\dorder$-th derivative it uses the
$\aorder + \dorder - 1$ integers around 0, with node 0 for even $\dorder$ and without it for odd $\dorder$, since
an odd derivative gives the centre no weight by antisymmetry (\cref{fig:stencil-nodes}). So \code{Central<2>}
evaluates $f$ three times for $f''$, four times for $f'''$ on the nodes $\pm 1, \pm 2$, five times for $f''''$:
\mnote{A rule that samples $x$ itself fails with \code{non_finite} where $f(x)$ is not finite: $\sin x / x$ at 0
works for $f'$ and $f'''$ with the central rules and not for $f''$.}
\begin{align*}
  f'''(x)  &\approx \frac{f(x + 2h) - 2 f(x + h) + 2 f(x - h) - f(x - 2h)}{2 h^3}, \\
  f''''(x) &\approx \frac{f(x + 2h) - 4 f(x + h) + 6 f(x) - 4 f(x - h) + f(x - 2h)}{h^4},
\end{align*}
the centred rules of $\order(h^2)$ in Chapra's tables (Chapter 23),\cite{chapra2015} whose $\order(h^4)$ rules are
\code{Central<4>}. The one-sided families work for every order too, \code{Forward<P>} on the nodes
$0, \ldots, \aorder + \dorder - 1$, and \code{Backward<P>} on their mirror image; their amplification grows
quickly with $\dorder$ (\cref{tab:weights-forward-fourth}), but exactly at an edge they are what works. The
library's three-point rule for $f''$ is evaluated in a slightly different form from \cref{eq:orders-second},
from the offsets of the points as they were actually rounded (\cref{sb:actual-offsets}); the two forms agree in
exact arithmetic whenever the points are exact. The complete tables for $\dorder = 2$ to 4 are in \cref{ch:tables}.

\section{Why accuracy falls with the order}\label{sec:orders-accuracy}

gives about \num{\valHeadlineFourthDigits} digits where the first gives \num{\valHeadlineFirstDigits}.\label{fig:v-curve-orders}}
\centering

\Cref{fig:v-curve-orders} is the V of \cref{ch:rounding} for each derivative order, again on $\mathrm{e}^x$ at
$x = 1$ so that the orders compare. The truncation branches on the right nearly coincide, because every derivative
of $\mathrm{e}^x$ is $\mathrm{e}^x$ and the error constants of the four rules, $\tfrac16$, $\tfrac1{12}$,
$\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac16$, differ little. The rounding branches on the left do not. A rounding error of
$\epsmach \abs{f}$ in each sample is divided by $h^{\dorder}$, so the branch has slope $-\dorder$: halving the step
doubles the rounding error of $f'$ and multiplies that of $f''''$ by sixteen. The error model of
\cref{sec:stencils-price} becomes
\begin{equation}
  E(h) = \errc\, h^{\aorder} \abs{f^{(\aorder + \dorder)}} + \ampl\, \epsmach \frac{\abs{f}}{h^{\dorder}},
  \label{eq:orders-model}
\end{equation}
and its minimum, worked out in \cref{sec:derivations-model}, is of order $\epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder + \dorder)}$.
For \code{Central<2>} in double that is $\epsmach^{2/3}$, $\epsmach^{1/2}$, $\epsmach^{2/5}$ and
$\epsmach^{1/3}$: \num{\valModelCentralTwoFirstDouble}, \num{\valModelCentralTwoSecondDouble},
\num{\valModelCentralTwoThirdDouble} and \num{\valModelCentralTwoFourthDouble}, about
\num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoFirstDouble}, \num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoSecondDouble},
\num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoThirdDouble} and \num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoFourthDouble} digits. The minima of
\cref{fig:v-curve-orders} rise accordingly, and the measured medians at the default step, in
\cref{fig:order-accuracy}, follow the model within its usual factor: \num{\valDoubleFourthCentralRelError}
for the fourth derivative against \num{\valExpCentralTwoRelError} for the first. These are the
\num{\valHeadlineFourthDigits} and \num{\valHeadlineFirstDigits} digits that \cref{ch:why} promised. What is lost to each
order, and why float cannot afford a fourth derivative at all, is quantified in \cref{sb:fourth-sensitivity}.

One caution about the word \emph{relative}. The model's rounding term is an absolute error,
$\ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / h^{\dorder}$, and dividing it by $\abs{f^{(\dorder)}}$ at the default step gives the
relative error $\ampl \epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder + \dorder)}$ times the factor
$\abs{f} / (\scale^{\dorder} \abs{f^{(\dorder)}})$. For $\mathrm{e}^x$ at 1 that factor is 1; the figures of
\cref{fig:order-accuracy} presuppose that $f$ varies on the scale $\scale$, so that $\abs{f^{(\dorder)}}$ is about
$\abs{f} / \scale^{\dorder}$. For the nitrogen example's second derivative at $T_0$ the factor
$\abs{\enth} / (\scale^2 \abs{\enth''})$ is \num{\valCpSecondRelativeFactor}, so the model's relative error is
$\ampl = 4$ times that times $\epsmach^{1/2}$, which is \num{\valCpSecondModelRelError}, and the measured
\num{\valCpSecondRelError} of \cref{sec:orders-thread} lies below it. Near a point where $f^{(\dorder)}$
vanishes the relative error grows without bound while the absolute error stays what the model says; there,
judge a result by its absolute error (\cref{sec:using-check}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:fourth-sensitivity]{Why the fourth derivative is so sensitive}
By \cref{eq:orders-model} the rounding error of a rule for $f^{(\dorder)}$ is $\ampl \epsmach \abs{f} /
h^{\dorder}$, so each halving of the step multiplies it by $2^{\dorder}$, and the balance with truncation is
reached at a larger step and a larger error: at the best step the error is of order
$\epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder + \dorder)}$, and the exponent falls from $\tfrac23$ for $f'$ to $\tfrac13$ for
$f''''$ with \code{Central<2>}. In double that is the difference between about
\num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoFirstDouble} digits and about \num{\valModelDigitsCentralTwoFourthDouble};
\code{Central<4>} does better, $\epsmach^{4/5}$ to $\epsmach^{1/2}$, at two more evaluations
(\cref{tab:default-steps}).

In float, $\epsmach$ is \num{\valEpsFloat} and the same exponents leave much less: $\epsmach^{2/3}$ is
\num{\valModelCentralTwoFirstFloat} for $f'$, and $\epsmach^{1/3}$ is \num{\valModelCentralTwoFourthFloat} for
$f''''$, two digits by the model. Measured on $\mathrm{e}^x$, the median error of \code{Central<2>} for the fourth
derivative in float is \num{\valFloatFourthCentralRelError}, and Ridders reaches \num{\valFloatFourthRiddersRelError}
with a relative estimate of \num{\valFloatFourthRiddersRelEstimate}: the estimate reports the noise honestly. The
third derivative is still usable, \num{\valFloatThirdRiddersRelError} with Ridders, which is why only the fourth
carries the warning of \cref{sec:orders-float}.

The step sensitivity grows the same way. The rounding term is proportional to $h^{-\dorder}$ and the truncation
term to $h^{\aorder}$: a step a factor 2 below the right one multiplies the rounding term by $2^{\dorder}$, and a
step a factor 2 above it multiplies the truncation term by $2^{\aorder}$. The default step assumes that $f$
varies on the scale $\scale$, and at the model's balance misjudging that scale by a factor 2 grows the total error
by at most \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFirst} for $f'$ and \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFourth} for $f''''$
with \code{Central<2>}, and \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralFourFourth} for \code{Central<4>} and $f''''$; the factor
16 that the library's documentation quotes for $f''''$ is the growth of the rounding term alone, $2^{\dorder}$.
A step on $f$'s own scale (\cref{sec:rounding-scale}) removes the sensitivity; Ridders removes the need to know
the scale at all.
\end{sidebar}

\section{The default step per order and its sensitivity}\label{sec:orders-step}

The default step balances the two terms of \cref{eq:orders-model} under the assumption that $f$ varies on the
scale $\scale = \max(\abs{x}, 1)$, with the constant dropped as in \cref{sb:step-constant}:
\begin{equation}
  h = \epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)}\, \scale .
  \label{eq:orders-step}
\end{equation}
The exponent is $1/(\aorder + \dorder)$, so the step grows with the order: for \code{Central<2>} in double it is
$\epsmach^{1/3}$, $\epsmach^{1/4}$, $\epsmach^{1/5}$ and $\epsmach^{1/6}$ times $\scale$, that is
\num{\valRootEpsThreeDouble}, \num{\valRootEpsFourDouble}, \num{\valRootEpsFiveDouble} and
\num{\valRootEpsSixDouble} at $\scale = 1$; at $T_0 = \SI{1000}{\kelvin}$ the step for $\enth''$ is
\SI{\valCpSecondStep}{\kelvin}, against \SI{\valCpDefaultStep}{\kelvin} for $\enth'$. \Cref{tab:default-steps}
lists every rule's exponent, step and model error. The constant that the model would add,
$(\dorder \ampl / (\aorder \errc))^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)}$, is \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoSecond} for $f''$ and
\num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFourth} for $f''''$ with \code{Central<2>}, and dropping it costs more by the model
as $\dorder$ grows; yet on test functions that vary faster than $\scale$, half the default step was better for
every order (\cref{sb:step-constant}), so the library keeps one convention and documents the sensitivity instead
(\cref{sec:derivations-model}).

That sensitivity is the practical point. A step off by a factor 2 from the right one grows the total error, by the
model, by up to \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFirst} for $f'$ and \num{\valMisjudgeCostCentralTwoFourth} for the
fourth derivative with \code{Central<2>} (\cref{sb:fourth-sensitivity}), and the rounding term alone by
$2^{\dorder}$. When $f$ varies on a scale $w$ much smaller than $\scale$, pass
$h = \epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} w$ as \cref{sec:rounding-scale} does for $f'$; and since the default step
depends on both the method and the order, generic code that varies $\dorder$ should call the default-step overload
rather than pass one $h$ for every order.

\section{The float warning}\label{sec:orders-float}

A fourth derivative in float is mostly rounding noise: \num{\valFloatFourthCentralRelError} relative with
\code{Central<2>} on $\mathrm{e}^x$, measured as a median, against \num{\valDoubleFourthCentralRelError} in
double (\cref{fig:order-accuracy}, \cref{sb:fourth-sensitivity}). The library says so at compile time: a call
with $\dorder = 4$ and a type whose epsilon is above $10^{-7}$, which is float, decimal32 or bfloat16 but not
double, selects a deprecated overload of \code{derivative}, and the compiler reports the deprecation at the
caller's line, with a message that names the remedy. The cure is to use double. Where a float fourth derivative is
wanted all the same, the warning can be silenced for one call or for the whole build, and the result is the same
with or without it (\cref{sec:api-warning}, which quotes the call and its numbers); what one gets is a value with
about two correct digits and, from Ridders, an estimate of \num{\valFloatFourthRiddersRelEstimate} relative that
says so (\cref{fig:order-accuracy}).

\section{Ridders for higher orders}\label{sec:orders-ridders}

Ridders' method extends to every order with the same tableau: the base of level $i$ is the \code{Central<2>} rule
for $\dorder$ at the step $h_0 / 2^i$, whose error expansion has even powers of $h$ only, so the extrapolation
formula of \cref{ch:ridders} applies unchanged. Three things differ. The default initial step is $\scale / 4$ for
$\dorder \ge 2$ rather than $\scale / 32$, because the base's rounding error grows as $1/h^{\dorder}$ and a larger
start pays. A level costs 2 evaluations for $f'$ and $f''$ and 4 for $f'''$ and $f''''$, plus $f(x)$ once per call
for the even orders, which is kept across restarts. And the gain over a stencil grows with the order: for the fourth
derivative in double, Ridders reaches a median of \num{\valDoubleFourthRiddersRelError} where \code{Central<2>}
reaches \num{\valDoubleFourthCentralRelError}, four more digits for a median of \num{\valRiddersEvalsMedianFourth}
evaluations over the random study of \cref{ch:ridders}, against five. \Cref{fig:order-accuracy} shows the three
methods side by side.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:actual-offsets]{The three-point $f''$ from the actual offsets}
The two-node rules of \cref{sb:actual-distance} divide by the distance between the points as evaluated, which
removes the rounding of the points. The library's three-point rule for $f''$ does the same with its two offsets.
With $x_+ = \fl(x + h)$ and $x_- = \fl(x - h)$ it forms $h_+ = x_+ - x$ and $h_- = x - x_-$ and evaluates
\[
  f''(x) \approx \frac{\dfrac{f(x_+) - f(x)}{h_+} - \dfrac{f(x) - f(x_-)}{h_-}}{(h_+ + h_-) / 2},
\]
the second divided difference of the three points where they actually are. The offsets $h_+$ and $h_-$ are exact
for every $0 < h \le \abs{x}$, in any radix, by Sterbenz's lemma (Theorem 4.3.1):\cite{sterbenz1974} for
$0 < h \le \abs{x} / 2$, $x_+$ and $x$ lie within a factor 2 of each other and have the same sign, and so do $x$
and $x_-$, so the subtractions round nothing; for $\abs{x} / 2 < h \le \abs{x}$ the subtraction $x - h$ is itself
exact by the same lemma, so $x_- = x - h$ and the offset $h_-$ is $h$, a representable number, with $x_- = 0$ at
$h = \abs{x}$, while $x_+$ stays within a factor 2 of $x$ as before. With exact offsets the formula is exact for every quadratic whatever the rounding of the points, and the
point-rounding term that a nominal-step formula carries, about $\epsmach \abs{x f'} / h^2$, drops out. This
mattered most for Ridders in float, whose second-derivative levels use the same form: in the prototype of the design
record (\code{docs/PHASE4_API.md}) the nominal step gave a worst case of 8.7 times the estimate, the actual offsets
1.4. The form also never computes $h^2$, which underflows in double for $h$ below about $10^{-154}$; every rule
with more nodes divides by $\den h$ and then by $h$ again, $\dorder - 1$ times, in the order that avoids a
spurious underflow or overflow (by $h$ first when $\den h > 1 > h$), for the same reason. Only
\code{Central<2>} for $f''$ uses the offsets; the rules with more nodes divide by the nominal step, as
\cref{sec:stencils-price} explains, and Ridders' levels for $f'''$ and $f''''$ account for the point rounding in
their floor instead (\cref{sb:floor-coverage}).
\end{sidebar}
