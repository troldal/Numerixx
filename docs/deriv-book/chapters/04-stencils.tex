\chapter{Higher-order and one-sided stencils}\label{ch:stencils}

\Cref{ch:taylor} built the forward and the central difference from Taylor's theorem. \Cref{ch:rounding} then showed
that the central difference reaches about \num{\valHeadlineFirstDigits} digits in double and no more: below its best
step, the rounding of $f$ takes over. This chapter asks what we gain by using more points. The answer is a higher
accuracy order. A higher order lets the step grow and the error fall, but it has a price: in rounding, in smoothness
and in evaluations. The chapter also answers a practical question that the central difference cannot. How do we
differentiate at the edge of a domain, where $f$ is defined on one side of $x$ only? The nitrogen example continues.
We compute $\cp = \dd \enth / \dd T$ of nitrogen at $T_0 = \SI{1000}{\kelvin}$, and at the lower end of the fit's
range.

\section{Stencils: nodes, weights and the moment conditions}\label{sec:stencils-moments}

We begin with what all rules have in common. Every rule of this book has the same shape. It samples $f$ at a few
points $x_k = x + n_k h$, and it combines the samples with fixed weights:
\begin{equation}
  f^{(\dorder)}(x) \approx \frac{1}{h^{\dorder}} \sum_k w_k\, f(x + n_k h).
  \label{eq:stencils-rule}
\end{equation}
\mnote{A \emph{node} is an integer offset $n_k$. The \emph{sample} is $f_k = f(x + n_k h)$. The \emph{stencil} is
the pattern of nodes, and a \emph{rule} is a stencil with its weights.}The integers $n_k$ are the nodes, and the
rational numbers $w_k$ are the weights. The derivative order $\dorder$ is 1 throughout this chapter. For example, the
central difference has the nodes $-1, 1$ with the weights $-\tfrac12, \tfrac12$. The forward difference has the
nodes $0, 1$ with the weights $-1, 1$. Two questions follow. Where do the weights come from? And how do we find the
weights for more nodes?

Taylor's theorem answers both. We expand each sample about $x$. The sample point lies $n_k h$ away from $x$, so
$f(x + n_k h) = \sum_j f^{(j)}(x)\, n_k^j h^j / j!$. Then we multiply each sample by its weight, add them up, and
collect the terms with the same power of $h$. This gives
\begin{equation}
  \sum_k w_k\, f(x + n_k h) = \sum_{j \ge 0} \frac{\mom{j}}{j!}\, h^j f^{(j)}(x),
  \qquad \mom{j} = \sum_k w_k\, n_k^j .
  \label{eq:stencils-moments}
\end{equation}
The numbers $\mom{j}$ are the \emph{moments} of the rule, and they decide everything. Read the right-hand side term
by term. We want the weighted sum to be $h^{\dorder} f^{(\dorder)}$ plus an error of order $h^{\dorder + \aorder}$.
That happens exactly when two things hold. First, $\mom{\dorder} = \dorder!$, so that the term with $j = \dorder$ is
$h^{\dorder} f^{(\dorder)}$. Second, every other moment up to $\mom{\dorder + \aorder - 1}$ vanishes. These are the
\emph{moment conditions}. The first moment that does not vanish, $\mom{\dorder + \aorder}$, gives the leading error
term of the rule, $\pm \errc\, h^{\aorder} f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}$. Its sign is the sign of
$\mom{\dorder + \aorder}$, which the tables of \cref{ch:tables} give. Its magnitude is
$\errc = \abs{\mom{\dorder + \aorder}} / (\dorder + \aorder)!$. And $\aorder$ is the accuracy order.

Let us work through an example: the four-point central rule for $f'$, the library's \code{Central<4>}. Its nodes are
$-2, -1, 1, 2$. We want a rule that is exact for the odd function $f(x + t) = t$ and gives zero for even functions.
Such a rule has antisymmetric weights: $w_{-1} = -a$, $w_1 = a$, $w_{-2} = -b$, $w_2 = b$. With these weights every
even moment vanishes by itself, because the terms for the nodes $n$ and $-n$ cancel. Two odd moments remain, and
they give two conditions: $\mom{1} = 2a + 4b = 1$ and $\mom{3} = 2a + 16b = 0$. Subtracting the first condition
from the second gives $12 b = -1$. So the solution is $a = \tfrac23$ and $b = -\tfrac{1}{12}$, and the rule with its
error is
\begin{equation}
  f'(x) = \frac{f(x - 2h) - 8 f(x - h) + 8 f(x + h) - f(x + 2h)}{12 h} + \frac{h^4}{30} f^{(5)}(\xi).
  \label{eq:stencils-central-four}
\end{equation}
The error comes from the fifth moment, $\mom{5} = 2a + 64 b = -4$. The signed coefficient is
$\mom{5} / 5! = -1/30$. So $\errc = 1/30$, and the rule's value is $f'$ minus that term. In other words, the rule
underestimates $f'$ where $f^{(5)} > 0$.\sidenote{The single intermediate point $\xi$ in the span of the stencil comes
from the interpolation error formula (\cref{sec:derivations-moments}). The next term is of order $h^6$, since all
even moments vanish.} Compared with the central difference, two more points have raised the order from 2 to 4.
\Cref{tab:weights-central-first} in \cref{ch:tables} lists the nodes, the weights, the amplification $\ampl$ and the
error constant $\errc$ of every central rule for $f'$. The general machinery that produces them, one formula over
the nodes, is in \cref{sb:moments}. One consequence of the moment conditions is worth keeping in mind. A rule of
order $\aorder$ is exact for every polynomial of degree up to $\dorder + \aorder - 1$, because all the derivatives
in its error terms vanish.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:moments]{The moment conditions and the Lagrange generator}
A rule for the $\dorder$-th derivative of accuracy order $\aorder$ on the nodes $n_1, \ldots, n_m$ satisfies
\[
  \mom{j} = \sum_k w_k n_k^j = 0 \quad \text{for } j \ne \dorder,\ 0 \le j \le \dorder + \aorder - 1,
  \qquad \mom{\dorder} = \dorder!,
\]
a linear system for the weights. The library does not solve it as a system, though. Instead it uses the Lagrange
formula (Burden et al.,\cite{burden2016} Section 4.1). Here is the idea. Write the points as $x + t h$, so that $t$ is
a scaled variable and the nodes are values of $t$. The polynomial $p$ that interpolates the samples at the nodes is
then $p(t) = \sum_k f_k L_k(t)$, with $L_k(t) = \prod_{j \ne k} (t - n_j) / (n_k - n_j)$. Its $\dorder$-th derivative
at $t = 0$ is $\sum_k f_k L_k^{(\dorder)}(0)$, so it gives the weights directly:
\[
  w_k = L_k^{(\dorder)}(0) = \dorder!\,[t^{\dorder}] \prod_{j \ne k} \frac{t - n_j}{n_k - n_j},
\]
where $[t^{\dorder}]$ takes the coefficient of $t^{\dorder}$. Why does this solve the moment conditions? The
interpolating polynomial $p$ reproduces every polynomial of degree below the number of nodes. So for $f = t^j$ the
weighted sum equals the $\dorder$-th derivative of $t^j$ at 0. That derivative is $\dorder!$ for $j = \dorder$ and 0
otherwise (\cref{sec:derivations-lagrange}), which is exactly what the moment conditions ask. The library's own
worked example is the node $-1$ of \code{Central<4>}. The product over the other nodes is
$\prod_{j \ne k} (t - n_j) = (t + 2)(t - 1)(t - 2) = t^3 - t^2 - 4t + 4$, and its $t^1$ coefficient is $-4$. The
denominator is $\prod (n_k - n_j) = (-1 + 2)(-1 - 1)(-1 - 2) = 6$. With $\dorder! = 1$, the weight is $-4$ over 6,
so $w = -2/3$, as in \cref{eq:stencils-central-four}.

The library stores every rule as integer numerators $c_k$ over one common denominator $\den$, so that
$w_k = c_k / \den$. For \code{Central<4>} the numerators are $1, -8, 8, -1$ over 12. With the integers, the moment
conditions read $\mom{\dorder} = \dorder!\,\den$. This is the form that \cref{ch:tables} tabulates, and it keeps the
weights exact in every real type. One \code{consteval} function, a function that the compiler evaluates while it
compiles the program, evaluates the Lagrange formula for every family and order. Each family only supplies its
nodes. The function works in exact rational arithmetic: fractions of \code{long long} integers, reduced with the
greatest common divisor. Then it puts all weights over their least common multiple, so that a table holds integers
only. The largest intermediate value it reaches is $12!$. This is the end node's product of node differences for
\code{Central<10>} and the fourth derivative ($12! \cdot 4!$ is a bound that is not reached). It is far inside the
range of \code{long long}. Every numerator and denominator must also fit in 24 bits, the size of the significand of
float. This keeps the weights exact in float as well as in double (the largest numerator is 6,222,216). The
generator checks the 24 bits before it narrows the integers to \code{int}. A type too narrow to hold a rule's
integers, such as half precision under \code{Central<10>}, is rejected by a constraint of \code{derivative}, rather
than used with rounded weights. The tests check every table in two ways: against the moment conditions, with a
moment function of their own, and against the published rows of Fornberg\cite{fornberg1988} and Chapra
(Chapter 23).\cite{chapra2015} They also use the exactness on polynomials. They take a point and a step that are
short binary fractions, so that no rounding occurs, and check that \code{Central<4>} on $x^4$ returns exactly 4,
both at compile time and at run time.
\end{sidebar}

\section{The order ladder}\label{sec:stencils-ladder}

Now we can compare the orders. \Cref{fig:order-ladder} puts the five central rules on one plot. From here to the end
of the chapter, the function is $\mathrm{e}^x$ at $x = 1$ rather than the nitrogen example. Every derivative of
$\mathrm{e}^x$ equals the function itself, so the errors of different orders are comparable. The nitrogen example
returns in \cref{sec:stencils-price}. There are three things to see in the figure.

On the right, truncation dominates. There each curve is a line whose slope is its order $\aorder$, because halving
the step divides the error by $2^{\aorder}$. The error of \code{Central<4>} falls sixteen times per halving of the
step, and that of \code{Central<10>} a thousand times. On the left, rounding dominates, and the curves nearly
coincide. The reason is that the rounding error is $\ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / h$, with the amplification
$\ampl = \sum_k \abs{w_k}$. Across the five rules, $\ampl$ grows only from \num{\valAmplCentralTwo} to
\num{\valAmplCentralTen} (\cref{tab:weights-central-first}). Finally, the minimum of each V-shaped curve slides up
that common rounding line, to the right. So a higher order does not lower the rounding branch. Instead it lets the
step grow, and a larger step means a smaller rounding error. The best error falls from about $\epsmach^{2/3}$ for
\code{Central<2>} to about $\epsmach^{10/11}$ for \code{Central<10>}. By the model, these are
\num{\valModelCentralTwoFirstDouble} and \num{\valModelCentralTenFirstDouble}. Measured at the default steps, the
medians over eleven points are \num{\valExpCentralTwoRelError} and \num{\valExpCentralTenRelError}
(\cref{fig:default-step-accuracy}).

The returns diminish, though. \Cref{fig:default-step-accuracy} shows the error at the default step against the
order. Going from \code{Central<2>} to \code{Central<4>} gains two decades, that is, a factor of 100 in the error.
Going from \code{Central<4>} to \code{Central<10>} gains less than two more. The whiskers show how much a single
point can differ from the median. There the error lies up to about \num{\valDefaultStepSpreadAbove} times above the
median, and up to \num{\valDefaultStepSpreadBelow} times below it, by luck in the rounding. So in double,
\code{Central<4>} is close to as good as it gets for $f'$. The higher orders earn their keep with wider types. There
$\epsmach$ is smaller, and $\epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder+1)}$ has further to fall (\cref{sec:using-types}).

\section{The price}\label{sec:stencils-price}

What does a higher order cost? We can read the answer off the error model. Here it is, written for any rule of order
$\aorder$ for $f'$:
\begin{equation}
  E(h) = \errc\, h^{\aorder} \abs{f^{(\aorder + 1)}} + \ampl\, \epsmach \frac{\abs{f}}{h},
  \label{eq:stencils-model}
\end{equation}
with the error constant $\errc$ and the amplification $\ampl$ defined from the weights, as above. The first term is
the truncation error, and the second the rounding error. The concrete model of \cref{ch:rounding} is the case
$\errc = \tfrac16$, $\ampl = 1$. Minimising $E(h)$ over $h$ gives the best step and the best error. The best step is
proportional to $\epsmach^{1/(\aorder + 1)}$, and the best error to $\epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder + 1)}$, as in
\cref{sb:step-constant} and \cref{sec:derivations-model}. The default step is $\epsmach^{1/(\aorder + 1)} \scale$,
with $\scale = \max(\abs{x}, 1)$. It leaves out the constant factor that $\errc$ and $\ampl$ would add. On the
nitrogen example we can see this in the steps themselves. \code{Central<2>} differentiates $\enth$ with a step of
\SI{\valCpDefaultStep}{\kelvin}, \code{Central<4>} with \SI{\valCpCentralFourStep}{\kelvin} and \code{Central<6>}
with \SI{\valCpCentralSixStep}{\kelvin}. The relative errors fall from \num{\valCpDefaultRelError} to
\num{\valCpCentralFourRelError} and \num{\valCpCentralSixRelError}:
\snippet{04-stencils-orders}{higher-order}

Three prices come with the larger stencil. The first is the rounding of the points. \Cref{eq:stencils-model}
accounts for the rounding of the samples $f_k$. But the points $x + n_k h$ are rounded too, each by up to
$\epsmach \abs{x} / 2$. A point that moves by that much moves its sample by about that amount times $f'$. Summed over
the stencil with the weights, this adds a third term, $(\ampl / 2)\, \epsmach \abs{x f'} / h$, of the same order as
the second. The two-node rules escape it. They divide by the distance between the points actually evaluated, rather
than by $2h$, and that removes the point rounding exactly (\cref{sb:actual-distance}). A rule with more nodes cannot
do this. Suppose it divided by the distance between its outer nodes. That would not remove their rounding; it would
quadruple it. Their weight in \code{Central<4>} is $1/12$, while the distance carries their rounding with weight
$4/12$. It would also leave the rounding of the inner nodes untouched. So the rule divides by a fixed multiple of
$h$, and it keeps the third term. The tests allow for that term (\cref{sec:guarantees-verification}).

The second price is smoothness. The error term in \cref{eq:stencils-central-four} holds only if $f$ has
$\aorder + \dorder$ continuous derivatives across the whole stencil. The stencil reaches $K h$ from $x$ on the sides
it samples, where $K$ is the largest node. Both $K$ and the default step grow with the order. On the nitrogen
example, \code{Central<6>} samples up to \SI{\valCpCentralSixStep}{\kelvin} times three on either side of $T_0$. A
kink or a range break anywhere in that span gives a wrong value, without any failure. A higher order makes this
worse, not better. \Cref{sec:using-pitfalls} measures it on the nitrogen example's own range break.

The third price is evaluations: $\aorder + \dorder - 1$ per call (\cref{tab:stencils-nodes}). Inside an iteration,
this is the whole cost of the derivative.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:richardson-disguise]{\code{Central<4>} is Richardson extrapolation in disguise}
Let $\Dc{h} = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / 2h$ be the central difference. Its error expansion has even powers only,
$\Dc{h} = f'(x) + a_2 h^2 + a_4 h^4 + \cdots$ (\cref{sb:remainder}). So halving the step divides the leading term by
4, and one combination removes it:
\[
  \frac{4 \Dc{h/2} - \Dc{h}}{3} = f'(x) - \frac{a_4}{4} h^4 + \cdots .
\]
Now write out the four samples, with $h' = h/2$. The term $4 \Dc{h'}$ is $8 (f(x + h') - f(x - h'))$ over $4h'$,
and $\Dc{h} = \Dc{2h'}$ is $f(x + 2h') - f(x - 2h')$ over the same $4h'$. The division by 3 turns $4h'$ into $12h'$,
and the combination becomes $(8 f(x + h') - 8 f(x - h') - f(x + 2h') + f(x - 2h')) / (12 h')$. This is
\cref{eq:stencils-central-four} exactly. So Richardson extrapolation at one fixed level is a stencil. It has the same
samples, the same weights and the same error, and it gives no information about how large that error is. What
Ridders' method (\cref{ch:ridders}) adds is not a better formula but a decision. It keeps halving the step and
extrapolating, until the entries stop changing by more than rounding. Then it reads an error estimate off the
changes. The price is that the number of samples is decided at run time.
\end{sidebar}

\section{One-sided rules}\label{sec:stencils-one-sided}

A central rule samples both sides of $x$. Sometimes $f$ exists on one side only: at the first temperature of a
correlation, at a phase boundary, or at $t = 0$ of a simulation. There the samples on the other side are either
unavailable or meaningless. The one-sided rules put all their nodes on one side. \code{Forward<P>} uses the nodes
$0, 1, \ldots, \aorder$ for $f'$, and never evaluates below $x$. \code{Backward<P>} is its mirror image. It uses the
nodes $-\aorder, \ldots, 0$, with the weights reversed and multiplied by $(-1)^{\dorder}$, and it never evaluates
above $x$ (\cref{fig:stencil-nodes}). The factor $(-1)^{\dorder}$ appears because the mirror image replaces $h$ by
$-h$, and the rule divides by $h^{\dorder}$. Both rules include $x$ itself. So they also serve at the upper end of a
range, where \code{Backward<P>} stays inside it, and for a function that is cheap to evaluate on one side only.

For a one-sided rule, each point buys one order. \code{Forward<P>} has $\aorder + 1$ weights and no symmetry to
help. So the $\aorder + 1$ moment conditions on $\mom{0}, \ldots, \mom{\aorder}$ fix the weights, and
$\mom{\aorder + 1}$ is the first moment that does not vanish. The result is order $\aorder$ from $\aorder + 1$ points.
A central rule gets its even moments for nothing, from antisymmetry. That is why it reaches the same order with one
point fewer, and why its orders are even.

The one-sided rules pay in their constants. The amplification $\ampl$ of \code{Forward<1>} to \code{Forward<6>} runs
from \num{\valAmplForwardOne} to \num{\valAmplForwardSix}. For the central rules it runs from \num{\valAmplCentralTwo}
to \num{\valAmplCentralTen}. The error constant of \code{Forward<2>} is \num{\valErrcForwardTwo}, against
\num{\valErrcCentralTwo} for \code{Central<2>} (\cref{tab:weights-forward-first}). The reason lies in the weights:
they alternate in sign and grow, so the samples cancel more and the rounding is amplified more. \Cref{fig:one-sided}
shows the effect. The truncation slopes are the same as those of the central rules of the same order, but the
rounding branch is shifted up. At the default step on $\mathrm{e}^x$, \code{Forward<2>} reaches
\num{\valExpForwardTwoRelError}, where \code{Central<2>} reaches \num{\valExpCentralTwoRelError}. \code{Forward<6>}
reaches \num{\valExpForwardSixRelError}, where \code{Central<6>} reaches \num{\valExpCentralSixRelError}
(\cref{fig:default-step-accuracy}). The one-sided rules stop at order 6. This is a design decision, which
\cref{sec:stencils-choosing} discusses together with the cap on the central rules.

The nitrogen example shows why the edge matters. Its Shomate fit is valid from \SI{500}{\kelvin} to
\SI{2000}{\kelvin}. But the formula, a polynomial plus a $1/T$ term, evaluates without complaint at
\SI{499}{\kelvin}. It returns a plausible number that describes nothing: that a formula can be evaluated does not
make it valid. A central rule at \SI{500}{\kelvin} samples below the range. It returns a derivative of the
extrapolated formula, with no sign that anything is wrong. The remedy is to make the domain explicit. The wrapper
below returns NaN outside the fit's range. Any rule that samples there then fails with \code{non_finite}, instead of
succeeding. The one-sided rules never sample there, and they succeed: \code{Forward<2>} with its three points, and
\code{Forward<4>} with five:

Measured, \code{Forward<2>} is \num{\valEdgeForwardTwoRelError} off the closed form, and \code{Forward<4>}
\num{\valEdgeForwardFourRelError}.

What about Ridders' method? It restarts below a step at which $f$ is not finite (\cref{ch:ridders}). But that does
not help exactly at an edge. With the same wrapper at \SI{500}{\kelvin}, every level samples below the range, so
each restart halves the step in vain. The result is \valEdgeRiddersStatus{} after \num{\valEdgeRiddersEvals}
evaluations, at the level cap. Near such an edge rather than at it, as for $\log x$ close to 0, the method recovers
(\cref{sec:ridders-trust}). Its one-sided form \code{ForwardRidders} samples at \SI{500}{\kelvin} and above only. It
returns the derivative there, with an error estimate (\cref{sec:ridders-onesided}). Below \SI{500}{\kelvin}, the
published enthalpy of nitrogen is a different fit, and the two fits do not meet. That makes the junction the range
break of \cref{sec:using-pitfalls}.

\section{Choosing P}\label{sec:stencils-choosing}

We can now say how to choose the order. As a rule, use \code{Central<2>} or \code{Central<4>} for double inside an
iteration. There two or four evaluations buy a relative error of about \num{\valExpCentralTwoRelError} or
\num{\valExpCentralFourRelError} with the default step. Use \code{Central<6>} and above only for wide types, where
$\epsmach$ is small enough for the order to pay; \cref{sec:using-types} uses \code{Central<6>} with fifty digits. Use
\code{Forward<2>} or \code{Forward<4>}, or their mirrors, exactly at an edge and nowhere else, since their constants
are larger. For a value that you report with an error estimate, use Ridders, or at an edge its one-sided forms.

The caps on the order are design decisions, not measured limits. The design record of the repository
(\code{docs/deriv/PHASE2_API.md}) records them. The central rules stop at order 10. To see what one more step up would
give, take the model's exponent alone. By it, a rule of order 12 would cut the error of $f'$ by a factor of about
\num{\valCapGainFactorCentralTwelveFirstDouble} in double, and \num{\valCapGainFactorCentralTwelveFirstMultiprecision}
in a 50-digit type. For the fourth derivative the factors are \num{\valCapGainFactorCentralTwelveFourthDouble} and
\num{\valCapGainFactorCentralTwelveFourthMultiprecision}. The one-sided rules stop at
order 6. There the amplification, already \num{\valAmplForwardSix} for $f'$, grows about as fast as the exponent pays
(\cref{sec:guarantees-limits}). The decision table by situation is \cref{tab:method-choice}, and every rule's nodes,
weights and constants are in \cref{ch:tables}.
