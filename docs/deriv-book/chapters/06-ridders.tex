\chapter{Ridders' extrapolation}\label{ch:ridders}

Every stencil of the last three chapters returns a number and nothing else. Its step is a guess: the default, or
your own. Whether the guess was good shows only when the answer is known from elsewhere. Ridders'
method,\cite{ridders1982} in the form that the library implements, removes the guess. It also adds the one thing
that a stencil cannot give: an estimate of its own error. Here is the idea in brief. The method evaluates the central
difference at a step. Then it halves the step, and halves it again. From the sequence of results it extrapolates to
the limit of zero step, and it keeps going until rounding stops the improvement. The size of the last improvement is
the estimate. The library offers the method as one more method tag, \code{derivative<Ridders>(fn, x)}, for every
derivative order. This chapter first explains the extrapolation and shows the tableau on the nitrogen example. Then
it says how to read \code{abs_error}, and when not to trust it. It ends with what the method costs, and what its
one-sided forms do at the edge of a domain.

\section{Richardson's idea}\label{sec:ridders-richardson}

We start from the central difference, $\Dc{h} = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / 2h$. Its error has an expansion in even
powers of the step (\cref{ch:taylor}, \cref{sb:remainder}):
\[
  \Dc{h} = f'(x) + a_2 h^2 + a_4 h^4 + a_6 h^6 + \cdots ,
\]
with coefficients $a_{2m}$ that depend on $f$ but not on $h$. Halving the step divides the leading error term by
4, since $(h/2)^2 = h^2 / 4$. So $\Dc{h}$ and $\Dc{h/2}$ are two measurements with a known relation between their
errors, and one combination of them cancels the leading term:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{4 \Dc{h/2} - \Dc{h}}{3} = f'(x) - \frac{a_4}{4} h^4 + \cdots .
  \label{eq:ridders-richardson}
\end{equation}
This is Richardson extrapolation,\cite{richardson1911,richardson1927} also called the deferred approach to the
limit. From two estimates with errors of order $h^2$, it makes one with an error of order $h^4$, without knowing
$a_2$. Applied once at a fixed step, it is nothing new: \cref{sb:richardson-disguise} showed that
\cref{eq:ridders-richardson} is the four-point rule \code{Central<4>}, written differently. What makes it a method is
repetition. The new estimate has the same form as the old one: an expansion in even powers, with the leading term
now $h^4$. So a second combination, with the factor $4^2 = 16$ in place of 4, removes that term. A third, with
$4^3$, removes the next, and so on. Each combination uses only values that the previous stage already has. Burden et
al.\cite{burden2016} (Section 4.2) present the tableau that this builds, and Sidi\cite{sidi2003} the general theory.

A word on what comes from where. Ridders' paper supplies the idea. The rules are the library's own: the ratio 2
between successive steps, a rounding floor, a stop after two levels without progress (a two-level stall), a final
check, and restarts. They were chosen on a prototype (\cref{sb:floor-coverage}), and every statistic in this chapter
is of them.

Now we organise the repeated extrapolation. Write $h_i = h_0 / 2^i$ for the step of level $i$, and $D_i = \Dc{h_i}$
for the base difference there. The extrapolated values form a triangular \emph{tableau}
(\cref{fig:ridders-tableau-diagram}), with one row per level and one column per cancelled term:
\begin{equation}
  \Aij{i}{0} = D_i, \qquad
  \Aij{i}{j} = \Aij{i}{j-1} + \frac{\Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1}, \quad 1 \le j \le i .
  \label{eq:ridders-tableau}
\end{equation}
Column $j$ is free of the error terms up to $h^{2j}$. The entry $\Aij{i}{j}$ is built from its left neighbour,
$\Aij{i}{j-1}$, and from that neighbour's parent one level up, $\Aij{i-1}{j-1}$. For $j = 1$ the formula gives
$D_i + (D_i - D_{i-1}) / 3 = (4 D_i - D_{i-1}) / 3$, which is Richardson's combination from the previous section,
with $h = h_{i-1}$. \Cref{eq:ridders-tableau} adds a correction to the left neighbour, rather than forming the
weighted combination of \cref{eq:ridders-richardson}. This correction form is the one that Burden et al. write, and
the one that the library computes, for reasons given in \cref{sb:difference-form}. The two forms are algebraically
the same.

\Cref{tab:ridders-tableau} is the tableau for the nitrogen example, $\cp = \dd \enth / \dd T$ at $T_0$. It starts
from the default initial step $h_0 = T_0 / 32$, about \SI{\valCpRiddersInitialStep}{\kelvin}. Read it by columns.
Column 0 is the plain central difference. At a step of thirty kelvin it is \num{\valTableauBaseFirstRelError} off,
and it gains a factor 4 per level, as \cref{ch:taylor} said it would. Column 1 is already \code{Central<4>} in
disguise, and it is far more accurate from its first entry on. Column 2 is at the rounding floor by level 3. From
there on, the entries of columns 2 and beyond agree with each other, and with the closed form, to the last digit
shown.

The lower table shows what the upper one cannot: the relative errors. Its last column holds the rounding floor $r_i$
of each level. This is the error that the rounding of the two samples alone imposes on $D_i$. It doubles per level,
because the step halves and the rounding error of a central difference is inversely proportional to the step. The
errors of the extrapolated entries stop falling once they reach the floor. From then on, they drift up with it.

\section{Reading \texttt{abs\_error}}\label{sec:ridders-estimate}

Where does the number after $\pm$ come from, and what does it mean? Every extrapolated entry of the tableau
carries an estimate of its own error,
\begin{equation}
  e_{i,j} = \max\bigl( \abs{\Aij{i}{j} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}},\ r_i \bigr), \qquad j \ge 1,
  \label{eq:ridders-estimate}
\end{equation}
that is, the change of the entry from its parent, but never less than the rounding floor $r_i$ of its level. The
change is the textbook estimate for successive approximations. Here is why it works. The parent $\Aij{i-1}{j-1}$ and
the entry's left neighbour $\Aij{i}{j-1}$ both lie in column $j - 1$, whose leading error term is of order $h^{2j}$,
and the parent has twice the step. So the parent's error is $4^j$ times that of the entry's left neighbour. The
entry's own error is smaller still. So the difference between entry and parent is nearly the parent's whole error,
and that overestimates the entry's error (\cref{sec:derivations-richardson}).

The floor is what keeps the estimate honest once truncation is gone. The entries of a converged column differ by
rounding alone. The smallest of several such differences is small by luck, not by accuracy. Without the floor, the
method would pick it, and report an error that it cannot vouch for. The floor of level $i$ is the rounding error of
the base difference: $(\epsmach \abs{f(x - h_i)} + \epsmach \abs{f(x + h_i)})$ divided by the distance between the
two points. It assumes that each value of \code{fn} is accurate to about $\epsmach$.

The method keeps the entry with the smallest estimate. It stops when two further levels produce no smaller one. The
reason is that each level doubles the floor, so more levels only add rounding. On the nitrogen example, these are the
last two levels of \cref{tab:ridders-tableau}. The returned \code{abs_error} is that smallest estimate, raised if
necessary to the distance between the returned entry and the latest diagonal entry $\Aij{i}{i}$. This last check
catches an entry that only looked good. On the nitrogen example the estimate is
\SI{\valCpRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}, against an actual error of
\SI{\valCpRiddersActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}. Note that the entries of the last level are more accurate than
the one returned. But their estimates are pinned at a larger floor, so they cannot say so. The method reports what it
can vouch for, not what happens to be best. The exact stopping rules, the restart rules and the constants are in
\cref{sb:floor-coverage}, with the evidence that they hold.

How good is the estimate? \Cref{fig:ridders-coverage} plots it against the actual error over the book's own study.
The study takes \num{\valRiddersFunctions} smooth functions with closed-form derivatives, and
\num{\valRiddersPointsPerFunction} random points each in $[0.2, 3]$, with the default initial step. A point above the
diagonal is a case where the estimate \emph{covered} the error: the estimate was at least as large as the actual
error. Here are the measured results. The estimate covers \SI{\valRiddersCoverageFirst}{\percent} of the first
derivatives and \SI{\valRiddersCoverageSecond}{\percent} of the second. The worst uncovered case has an error of
\num{\valRiddersWorstRatioFirst} times its estimate for the first derivative, and \num{\valRiddersWorstRatioSecond}
for the second. The median ratio of error to estimate is \num{\valRiddersMedianRatioFirst} and
\num{\valRiddersMedianRatioSecond}. So the typical estimate is an overestimate by a factor of ten or so.

Two things that the figure does not say must be said. First, the estimate is not a bound. Second, the study's scope
is smooth functions, with no noise, and for $f'$ no singularity within the initial step. For the higher orders, the
larger initial step does reach a singularity at small $x$. The method survives it by restarting, or, where the
samples stay finite, by taking more levels (\cref{sb:floor-coverage}). What happens outside that scope is the subject
of the next section.

\section{When not to trust it}\label{sec:ridders-trust}

The estimate rests on assumptions, and this section goes through the situations that break them: a non-finite
value, samples that lie, a function less accurate than $\epsmach$, and a jump or a kink. For each, it says what to do.

\paragraph{A non-finite value: the edge of a domain.} Near the edge of a domain, Ridders' method still works, at a
higher cost. Exactly at an edge it does not. There, its one-sided forms (\cref{sec:ridders-onesided}) or the
one-sided stencils of \cref{sec:stencils-one-sided} are the tool. The initial step for $f'$ is $\scale / 32$, with
$\scale = \max(\abs{x}, 1)$, and near an edge it may reach across the edge. The method then sees a NaN or an
infinity. It discards the whole tableau, since every earlier level's interval contained the bad point too, and
restarts from half the failing step. Take $\log x$ at $x = 0.01$. The step $1/32$ samples below zero, and so does
$1/64$. So two restarts precede the tableau that converges:
\snippet{06-ridders-edge}{edge-restart}
Ridders returns \num{\valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthValue}, accurate to \num{\valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthRelError}
relative and covered by its estimate. It needs \num{\valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthEvals} evaluations, against
\num{\valRiddersEdgeEvalsInterior} for $\log x$ away from its edge. Four of the extra evaluations are the two failed
levels. The rest come from a tableau that starts at $1/128$, nearly the size of $x$, and so needs more levels to
converge.

\Cref{fig:ridders-edge} measures how far this goes. Each further halving of $x$ adds one failed level, which is two
evaluations. The level budget of \num{\valRiddersLevelCap} is used up from
$x = 2^{-\num{\valRiddersEdgeCapExponent}}$ on. Ten correct digits hold down to $x = \num{\valRiddersEdgeTenDigits}$.
A value is still returned down to $x = \num{\valRiddersEdgeLimit}$, with the estimate risen to
\num{\valRiddersEdgeLimitRelEstimate} of the value. Below that, the result is \code{non_finite}. As a rule, this has
two readings. First, a value is returned as long as the nearest bad point of $f$ is about $2^{-34} \scale$ away for
$f'$, $2^{-31} \scale$ for $f''$, and $2^{-30} \scale$ for $f'''$ and $f''''$. (For the last two, the initial step is
$\scale / 4$, and the rules reach $2h$.) These distances are derived from the initial step and the level cap, and
measured in \cref{sec:guarantees-limits}. Second, for ten digits, keep the bad point at least
$2^{-\num{\valRiddersEdgeTenDigitsExponent}} \scale$ away (measured for $f'$).

The stencil in the same example does not fail, since its default step of $\epsmach^{1/3}$ never reaches zero. But its
relative error is \num{\valEdgeCentralAtOneHundredthRelError}. This is the truncation error: for $\log x$,
$f''' = 2/x^3$ and $f' = 1/x$, so the relative truncation error $(h^2/6) f''' / f'$ is $h^2 / 3x^2$. The step is
scaled by $\scale = 1$ rather than by $x$: the small-$\abs{x}$ case of \cref{sec:rounding-scale}. Ridders handles
that case by adapting the step.

\paragraph{A pole between the samples, or aliasing.} The samples can be finite and still lie. A pole of $f$ between
two sample points gives finite differences that converge to nothing. And a function that oscillates faster than the
initial step can vanish at every sample. The method has a test for this. The test looks for a result that has at
most two correct bits by its own estimate, while that estimate is far above rounding. When the test fires, the method
restarts at half the smallest step it tried (\cref{sb:aliasing}). That restart descends in the same way as the one
after a non-finite value, so a pole close to $x$ is resolved. The tests place poles $10^{-3}$ ahead of 0 and
$10^{-4}$ behind it, far inside the limits above. There, \num{\valPoleCovered} of the \num{\valPoleCases} results
for $f'$ to $f''''$ are covered, from \num{\valPoleRelErrorMin} to \num{\valPoleRelErrorMax} off. A pole much closer
than that gives no \code{non_finite}, but a wrong value. In every case measured, the estimate flagged that value
(\cref{sec:guarantees-limits}). So test \code{abs_error}.

\paragraph{A function less accurate than $\epsmach$.} The floor assumes that \code{fn} is accurate to about
$\epsmach$. A function that carries the noise $\noise$ of an iterative solver (\cref{sec:rounding-noise}) breaks that
assumption, and the estimate becomes optimistic. We measured this over the study's \num{\valNoiseRiddersCases}
random points of $\mathrm{e}^x$, with the values perturbed by $\noise = 10^{-8}$. The coverage falls to
\SI{\valNoiseEightRiddersCoverage}{\percent}, with a worst ratio of \num{\valNoiseEightRiddersWorstRatio}
(\cref{fig:noise}). So pass an initial step on the scale of the noise, and do not read the estimate as a bound.

\paragraph{A jump or a kink inside the span.} A range break within $h_0$ of $x$ does not produce a non-finite value.
So Ridders returns a value for the wrong function. Where $f$ itself jumps, it also returns a large estimate. The base
differences then converge like $\order(h)$ rather than $\order(h^2)$, and the entries keep changing. On the nitrogen
example's own range break, the stencils return errors of order one in silence. Ridders' relative estimate there is
\num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelEstimate}, ten orders of magnitude above the \num{\valKinkOutsideRiddersRelEstimate}
that it reports \SI{\valKinkOutsideDelta}{\kelvin} from the break (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}, \cref{fig:kink}). The
estimate flags a jump; the stencil does not.

A kink at $x$ itself is different. A kink is a jump in $f'$ with $f$ continuous, and at $x$ itself it shows no
symptom at all. The central difference of $c \abs{t}$ is exactly zero at every step, since $c \abs{h} = c \abs{-h}$.
So every central rule returns the mean of the two one-sided slopes, plus the smooth part's own error. Ridders'
tableau converges to that mean, with its estimate at the floor, and nothing in the samples can show the kink. Know
the branch boundaries, and differentiate the branch you mean (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls}).

\cref{tab:accuracy-matrix}). And it gives an estimate.

\section{Stencil or Ridders}\label{sec:ridders-choice}

Why does only Ridders estimate its error? A stencil could bound its truncation error only with extra evaluations,
at a second step or at a higher order. And the rounding part of its error alone is smallest exactly where truncation
is largest. An estimate that costs nothing would be worth nothing. So the library leaves a stencil's
\code{abs_error} empty, and code that needs an estimate asks for Ridders.

The choice between the two follows from that. Inside an iteration, the derivative is recomputed many times, and a
Newton step tolerates an approximate slope. There a stencil with the default step is the right tool: two or four
evaluations, a relative error of about \num{\valExpCentralTwoRelError} or \num{\valExpCentralFourRelError}, and no
decisions. For a value that is reported, compared or published, use Ridders. It takes several times the
evaluations. In return it gives about three more digits than \code{Central<2>} (\num{\valHeadlineRiddersFirstDigits}
against \num{\valHeadlineFirstDigits}), as many as \code{Central<4>}, and a number that says how far to trust it. (A
stencil's result can be checked as in \cref{sec:using-check}.)

Sometimes $f$ is undefined or different on one side of $x$: at a domain edge, at the end of a correlation's range,
or when you want the right-hand or the left-hand slope at a kink. For these cases Ridders' method has one-sided
forms. \code{ForwardRidders} extrapolates the \code{Forward<1>} rule of order $\dorder$. For $f'$ that rule is the
quotient $(f(x + h_i) - f(x)) / h_i$, computed over the actual distance and offsets
(\cref{sb:actual-distance,sb:actual-offsets}). \code{ForwardRidders} evaluates \code{fn} at $x$ and above only,
restarts included. \code{BackwardRidders} applies it to $f(-t)$ at $-x$, and negates the value for odd $\dorder$. The
negation undoes the chain rule: the $\dorder$-th derivative of $f(-t)$ is $(-1)^{\dorder}$ times that of $f$. So
\code{BackwardRidders} samples at $x$ and below, with the same accuracy, cost and limits.

How does the one-sided tableau differ? The forward rule's error has every power of the step,
$b_1 h + b_2 h^2 + \cdots$, not just the even ones. So column $j$ removes the term in $h^j$, which halving the step
divides by $2^j$. The tableau therefore divides by $2^j - 1$ instead of $4^j - 1$, and it gains one order per level
instead of two. This is the general case of Burden et al.\cite{burden2016} (Section 4.2;
\cref{sec:derivations-richardson}). The smaller divisors amplify rounding more. So the floor of an entry is carried
through the tableau, $r_{i,j} = (2^j r_{i,j-1} + r_{i-1,j-1}) / (2^j - 1)$ from $r_{i,0} = r_i$, rather than taken
from its level. This formula adds the floors of the two entries that the entry is built from, with the weights that
it gives them. The rest, restarts and cap included, is Ridders' method as described.

The default initial step is $\scale / \num{\valForwardRiddersDivisor}$ for every order. It was chosen on a prototype
sweep (the design record). A call evaluates $f(x)$ once, and $\dorder$ points per level. That is at most
\num{\valForwardRiddersMaxEvalsFirst} to \num{\valForwardRiddersMaxEvalsFourth} evaluations, and typically
\num{\valForwardRiddersEvalsMedianFirst} for $f'$ and \num{\valForwardRiddersEvalsMedianFourth} for $f''''$.

Using one side only has a price. We measured it on the study of \cref{fig:ridders-coverage}: the same smooth
functions and points, finite above every $x$, with no noise, in double. The median relative error runs from
\num{\valForwardRiddersMedianRelErrorFirst} for $f'$ to \num{\valForwardRiddersMedianRelErrorFourth} for $f''''$.
That is \num{\valForwardRiddersAccuracyFactorFirst} to \num{\valForwardRiddersAccuracyFactorFourth} times Ridders'
error on the same cases. The estimate covers \SI{\valForwardRiddersCoverageMin}{\percent} to
\SI{\valForwardRiddersCoverageMax}{\percent} of them, and the worst error is \num{\valForwardRiddersWorstRatioMax}
times its estimate. A pole just ahead of $x$, inside the span that the rule samples, can defeat it for $f''''$,
unflagged, well inside the reach given below (\cref{sec:guarantees-ridders}). So where $f$ is smooth on both sides,
use Ridders. That holds near an edge too, where the non-finite values guide its restarts. Here is the one-sided form
at the nitrogen fit's lower end, with the wrapper of \cref{sec:stencils-one-sided}:

Ridders returns \valEdgeRiddersStatus{} after \num{\valEdgeRiddersEvals} evaluations. The one-sided form starts
from an initial step of about \SI{\valEdgeForwardRiddersInitialStep}{\kelvin}. It returns $\cp$
\num{\valEdgeForwardRiddersRelError} off, with an estimate of \num{\valEdgeForwardRiddersRelEstimate}, after

off, with an estimate of \num{\valEdgeForwardRiddersSecondRelEstimate}, after \num{\valEdgeForwardRiddersSecondEvals}.
All these errors and estimates are relative.

The reach of the one-sided form is limited in another way. Suppose that $f$ varies on a scale far below the initial
step, as $\log x$ near 0 does. A one-sided base then sees no non-finite value, only untrusted results. It restarts at
half the smallest step it tried, and beyond its reach the levels run out first (\cref{fig:ridders-onesided-reach}).
Take $\log x$ at $x = 2^{-k}$. There $f'$ has ten digits to $k = \num{\valForwardRiddersReachTenDigitsExponentFirst}$.
At the documented reach, $k = 30$, $f'$ is \num{\valForwardRiddersReachRelErrorAtThirtyFirst} off, and $f''''$
\num{\valForwardRiddersReachRelErrorAtThirtyFourth}. The estimate covers the growing error up to
$k = \num{\valForwardRiddersReachCoveredExponentFirst}$ for $f'$ (\num{\valForwardRiddersReachCoveredExponentFourth}
for $f''''$). From $k = \num{\valForwardRiddersReachFlaggedExponentFirst}$
(\num{\valForwardRiddersReachFlaggedExponentFourth}) to the end of the study, $k = \num{\valForwardRiddersReachLastK}$,
the wrong result is flagged: its estimate is at least a quarter of its value. At
$k = \num{\valForwardRiddersReachSilentExponentFirst}$, between these two ranges, $f'$ is
\num{\valForwardRiddersReachSilentRelErrorFirst} off with an estimate of
\num{\valForwardRiddersReachSilentRelEstimateFirst}. That result is neither covered nor flagged. As a rule, keep
$f$'s nearest feature at least about $2^{-30} \scale$ from $x$, or pass an initial step on its scale.

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:difference-form]{Burden's difference form of the tableau}
\Cref{eq:ridders-richardson} generalises to the weighted combination
$(4^j \Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}) / (4^j - 1)$ for column $j$. Burden et al.\cite{burden2016} (Section 4.2) write
the equal correction form,
\[
  \Aij{i}{j} = \Aij{i}{j-1} + \frac{\Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1},
\]
and the library computes that form. There are three reasons.

First, exact agreement. When the two parents agree exactly, the correction is 0, and the entry equals them bit for
bit. The stop on exact agreement relies on this. The product form could round.

Second, overflow. The correction is a small quantity divided by a number above 1, so it cannot overflow where the
entries themselves are finite. In contrast, $4^j \Aij{i}{j-1}$ can exceed the largest finite value in float from
$j = 7$ on, for a derivative near $2^{115}$: then $4^7 \cdot 2^{115} = 2^{129}$, while the largest float is just
below $2^{128}$. The library still checks the one subtraction and the one addition before forming them, since two
finite entries of opposite sign can differ by more than the largest value. It treats a failure as a failed level.

Third, memory. Only the previous row is needed. The tableau is two arrays of \num{\valRiddersLevelCap} entries. They
are filled from \code{T(0)}, so that the real type need not be default-constructible (that is, constructible without
an initial value), and they are swapped after each level.

A last detail concerns the factors. The factors $4^j$ are built by repeated multiplication in the real type, and
they are exact in binary. The number $4^j - 1$ needs $2j$ bits, so it rounds once $2j$ exceeds the
significand. That happens from $j = 13$ in float and $j = 27$ in double, and far beyond the cap in a 50-digit type.
The rounding is at most half an epsilon, which does not matter.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:floor-coverage]{How Ridders stops, and how often its estimate holds}
\emph{The rules}, as the code has them. Level $i$ is the \code{Central<2>} rule for the order, at
$h_i = h_0 / 2^i$. Before the level is evaluated, the method checks three things: that $h_i$ is positive; that
$x \pm h_i$ differ from $x$ (and, for $f'''$ and $f''''$, that $x \pm 2 h_i$ differ from $x \pm h_i$); and that no
point can overflow. At the resolution of $x$, where the step no longer moves the points, the tableau simply ends.
Each extrapolated entry gets the estimate $e_{i,j}$ of \cref{eq:ridders-estimate}, with the floor $r_i$ of its level.
The floor depends on the order. For $f'$ it is the rounding of the two samples over their distance. For $f''$ it is
the same, from the three samples and the two offsets. For $f'''$ and $f''''$ it is the rounding of the samples plus
the rounding of the points, $\tfrac12 \epsmach (\abs{x} + 2h)\, \abs{f'} \sum_k \abs{c_k} / (\den h^{\dorder})$. Here
the $c_k$ are the integer numerators over the denominator $\den$, and $f'$ is taken from the samples at $x \pm h$.
The point term is needed because those rules divide by the nominal step.

The best entry is the one with the smallest estimate since the last restart. The tableau stops in four cases: when an
entry equals its parent exactly (nothing is left to learn, so that entry is best, with the floor as its estimate);
when two consecutive levels bring no smaller estimate; at the resolution of $x$; or when the level budget runs out.
The result is the best entry, with $\errest = \max(e_{\mathrm{best}}, \abs{A_{\mathrm{best}} - \Aij{i}{i}})$ for the
latest diagonal entry. A result needs at least two successful levels since the last restart.

\emph{Restarts.} A level fails in three cases: if \code{fn} is not finite at a point; if a difference, quotient or
tableau entry would overflow; or if the points would. The tableau, its best entry and the stall counter are then
discarded, and a new tableau starts from half the failing step. A result is \emph{untrusted} if two conditions hold.
The first is $\errest \ge \abs{A_{\mathrm{best}}} / 4$: since $1/4 = 2^{-2}$, the result has at most two correct
bits. The second is $\errest / 64 > r_{\mathrm{best}}$: the error is more than rounding explains. The method then
restarts at half the smallest step it tried, as after a failed level. It keeps the untrusted result as a fallback,
which it returns if the budget runs out before a trusted one appears. A tableau that ended at the resolution of $x$
is returned as it is, since no restart could go lower. A derivative that is exactly 0 has an estimate near its floor,
and is not affected. The budget is \num{\valRiddersLevelCap} levels in all. For the even orders, $f(x)$ is evaluated
once and kept across restarts. If it is not finite, the result is \code{non_finite} after that one evaluation.

\emph{Where the constants come from.} Several constants were chosen with a prototype on random cases: the ratio 2,
the two-level stall, the factor 1 on the floor, the factors 4 and 64 of the untrusted test, and the cap of 32. The
design record in the repository, \code{docs/deriv/PHASE3_API.md} to \code{docs/deriv/PHASE5_API.md}, gives the
evidence for each. Without the floor, the estimate fell below the actual error in more than half of the prototype's
cases. A one-level stall let a Runge-function case stop early. An untrusted test at $\abs{A}$ (factor 1) instead of
$\abs{A} / 4$ left poles between samples uncovered. Factors between 16 and 256 over the floor gave identical results.

The book's own evidence is the study of \cref{fig:ridders-coverage}. It takes \num{\valRiddersFunctions} functions
($\mathrm{e}^x$, $\sin x$, $\log x$, $\sqrt{x}$, $1/x$, the Runge function $1/(1 + 25x^2)$, $x^5$,
$\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$ and $\tanh x$) at \num{\valRiddersPointsPerFunction} random points each in $[0.2, 3]$, with the
default initial step, no noise, and no singularity within $h_0$ for $f'$. Here are the measured results. The
coverage is \SI{\valRiddersCoverageFirst}{\percent} for $f'$ (\num{\valRiddersUncoveredFirst} of
\num{\valRiddersCases} cases uncovered) and \SI{\valRiddersCoverageSecond}{\percent} for $f''$
(\num{\valRiddersUncoveredSecond}). The worst ratios of error to estimate are \num{\valRiddersWorstRatioFirst} and
\num{\valRiddersWorstRatioSecond}, and the median ratios \num{\valRiddersMedianRatioFirst} and
\num{\valRiddersMedianRatioSecond}.

The uncovered cases have a shape in common. For $f'$, \num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainCountFirst} of the
\num{\valRiddersUncoveredFirst} are \valRiddersUncoveredMainFunctionFirst{} at $x$ between
\num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainMinXFirst} and \num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainMaxXFirst} (the other:
\valRiddersUncoveredOthersFirst). For $f''$, all \num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainCountSecond} are
\valRiddersUncoveredMainFunctionSecond{} at $x$ between \num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainMinXSecond} and
\num{\valRiddersUncoveredMainMaxXSecond}. There the derivatives grow like $(2x)^k$, and the first levels are not yet
in the asymptotic regime, where the leading error term dominates. So the change from the parent undershoots the
entry's error. A function whose derivatives grow geometrically on the scale of $h_0$ is where the estimate is
tightest. A smaller initial step usually restores the margin. A quarter of the default initial step covers
\num{\valRiddersMarginQuarterCovered} of the study's \num{\valRiddersMarginCases} uncovered cases (all four orders),
and a sixty-fourth covers \num{\valRiddersMarginSixtyFourthCovered}.

There were no restarts for $f'$, and \num{\valRiddersRestartedSecond} for $f''$. These are the cases of $\log x$ and
$\sqrt{x}$ below $x = 0.25$, where $h_0 = \scale / 4$ reaches below zero and the first levels are NaN. (There
$\scale = 1$, so $h_0 = 1/4$ is larger than $x$.) $1/x$ at the same points does not restart. Its pole lies between
finite samples, so the first levels are simply wrong, not NaN. The tableau takes more levels, and converges from
the later ones, accurately.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:aliasing]{Aliasing: when the samples lie}
Take $f(x) = \sin(64 \pi x)$ at $x = 0$, whose derivative is $64\pi$. The default initial step is $1/32$, and
$64 \pi / 32 = 2\pi$. So the first level samples $\sin(\pm 2\pi)$, and the second $\sin(\pm \pi)$. Every sample is
zero up to rounding. The two base differences nearly agree, and the first extrapolated entry is a confident 0 with
a tiny estimate. What saves the call is the sequence. The third level samples $\sin(\pm \pi / 2) = \pm 1$, and the
diagonal entries jump to numbers of order 100, while the best entry stays near 0. The final check raises the
estimate to the distance from the latest diagonal entry. Now the untrusted test fires, and the method restarts at
half the smallest step it tried. The second tableau converges to $64\pi$ (measured: \valAliasingResult{}, after
\num{\valAliasingEvals} evaluations).

The design record names the case that cannot be caught: samples that are exactly periodic \emph{and} lie on a
polynomial. An example is $\cos(64 \pi x)$ at 0 for $f''$, whose first samples are all exactly 1. Two entries agree
exactly, and the method stops with the floor as its estimate. That fools every sampling method. The remedy is a step
on the scale of the period.
\end{sidebar}
