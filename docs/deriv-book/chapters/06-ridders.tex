\chapter{Ridders' extrapolation}\label{ch:ridders}

Every stencil of the last three chapters returns a number and nothing else. Its step is a guess, the default or
the user's, and whether the guess was good shows only when the answer is known from elsewhere. Ridders'
method\cite{ridders1982} removes the guess and adds the one thing a stencil cannot give: an estimate of its own
error. It evaluates the central difference at a step, halves the step, and again, extrapolating the sequence to
the limit of zero step until rounding stops the improvement; the size of the last improvement is the estimate.
The library offers it as one more method tag, \code{derivative<Ridders>(fn, x)}, for every derivative order.
This chapter explains the extrapolation, shows the tableau on the thread, says how to read \code{abs_error} and
when not to trust it, and what it all costs.

\section{Richardson's idea}\label{sec:ridders-richardson}

The central difference $\Dc{h} = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / 2h$ has an error expansion in even powers of the step
(\cref{ch:taylor}, \cref{sb:remainder}):
\[
  \Dc{h} = f'(x) + a_2 h^2 + a_4 h^4 + a_6 h^6 + \cdots ,
\]
with coefficients $a_{2m}$ that depend on $f$ but not on $h$. Halving the step divides the leading error term by
4, so $\Dc{h}$ and $\Dc{h/2}$ are two measurements with a known relation between their errors, and one
combination cancels the term:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{4 \Dc{h/2} - \Dc{h}}{3} = f'(x) - \frac{a_4}{4} h^4 + \cdots .
  \label{eq:ridders-richardson}
\end{equation}
This is Richardson extrapolation,\cite{richardson1911,richardson1927} the deferred approach to the limit: from two
estimates of order $h^2$, one of order $h^4$, without knowing $a_2$. Applied once at a fixed step it is nothing
new; \cref{sb:richardson-disguise} showed that \cref{eq:ridders-richardson} is the four-point rule
\code{Central<4>} written differently. What makes it a method is repetition: the new estimate has the same form,
an expansion in even powers with the leading term now $h^4$, so a second combination with the factor
$4^2 = 16$ removes that term, a third with $4^3$ the next, and so on, each from values the previous stage already
has. Burden et al.\cite{burden2016} give the textbook form and Sidi\cite{sidi2003} the general theory.

\section{The tableau in one picture}\label{sec:ridders-tableau}

\begin{figure}

of each level. A diagram without data.\label{fig:ridders-tableau-diagram}}
\centering
\input{figures/06-ridders-tableau-diagram}
\end{figure}

Write $h_i = h_0 / 2^i$ for the step of level $i$ and $D_i = \Dc{h_i}$ for the base difference there. The
extrapolated values form a triangular tableau (\cref{fig:ridders-tableau-diagram}), one row per level and one
column per cancelled term:
\begin{equation}
  \Aij{i}{0} = D_i, \qquad
  \Aij{i}{j} = \Aij{i}{j-1} + \frac{\Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1}, \quad 1 \le j \le i .
  \label{eq:ridders-tableau}
\end{equation}
Column $j$ is free of the terms up to $h^{2j}$, and the entry $\Aij{i}{j}$ is built from its left neighbour and
that neighbour's parent one level up. The form of \cref{eq:ridders-tableau}, a correction added to the left
neighbour rather than the weighted combination of \cref{eq:ridders-richardson}, is Burden's difference form,
which the library uses for reasons given in \cref{sb:difference-form}; the two are algebraically the same.

\section{Reading \texttt{abs\_error}}\label{sec:ridders-estimate}

Where does the number after $\pm$ come from, and what does it mean? Every extrapolated entry of the tableau
carries an estimate of its own error,
\begin{equation}
  e_{i,j} = \max\bigl( \abs{\Aij{i}{j} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}},\ r_i \bigr), \qquad j \ge 1,
  \label{eq:ridders-estimate}
\end{equation}
the change of the entry from its parent, but never less than the rounding floor $r_i$ of its level. The change
is the textbook estimate of successive approximations: the parent's error is larger than the entry's by about a
factor $4^j$, so the difference between them is nearly the parent's whole error, which overestimates the entry's
(\cref{sec:derivations-richardson}). The floor is what keeps the estimate honest once truncation is gone: the
entries of a converged column differ by rounding alone, the smallest of several such differences is small by
luck rather than by accuracy, and without the floor the method would pick it and report an error it cannot
vouch for. The floor of level $i$ is the rounding error of the base difference, $(\epsmach \abs{f(x - h_i)} +
\epsmach \abs{f(x + h_i)})$ divided by the distance between the two points, under the assumption that each
value of \code{fn} is accurate to about $\epsmach$.

The method keeps the entry with the smallest estimate, and stops when two further levels produce no smaller one,
because each level doubles the floor, so more levels only add rounding (on the thread, levels 5 and 6 in
\cref{tab:ridders-tableau}). The returned \code{abs_error} is that smallest estimate, raised if necessary to the
distance between the returned entry and the latest diagonal entry $\Aij{i}{i}$, which catches an entry that only
looked good. On the thread the estimate is \SI{\valCpRiddersEstimate}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin} against an
actual error of \SI{\valCpRiddersActual}{\joule\per\mole\per\kelvin}; note that the entries of level 6 are
more accurate than the one returned, but their estimates, pinned at a larger floor, cannot say so, and the
method reports what it can vouch for rather than what happens to be best. The exact stopping rules, the restart
rules and the constants are in \cref{sb:floor-coverage}, with the evidence that they hold.

\section{When not to trust it}\label{sec:ridders-trust}

\paragraph{A non-finite value: the edge of a domain.} The initial step is $\scale / 32$ for $f'$ with
$\scale = \max(\abs{x}, 1)$, which near a domain edge may reach across it. The method then sees a NaN or an
infinity, discards the tableau and restarts from half the failing step, since every earlier level's interval
contained the bad point too. $\log x$ at $x = 0.01$ is the header's own example: the step $1/32$ samples
below zero, and so does $1/64$, so two restarts precede the tableau that converges:
\snippet{06-ridders-edge}{edge-restart}
Ridders returns \num{\valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthValue} to \num{\valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthRelError} relative,
covered by its estimate, after \valRiddersEdgeAtOneHundredthEvals{} evaluations against \valCpRiddersEvals{} in
the interior: a restart costs about two evaluations per halving. \Cref{fig:ridders-edge} measures how far this
goes: each halving of $x$ costs one more restart, the level budget of \valRiddersLevelCap{} is used up from
$x = 2^{-\valRiddersEdgeCapExponent}$, ten correct digits hold down to $x = \num{\valRiddersEdgeTenDigits}$, a value
is returned down to $x = \num{\valRiddersEdgeLimit}$, and below that the result is \code{non_finite}. As a rule,
Ridders with the default step needs the nearest bad point of $f$ about $2^{-34} \scale$ away for $f'$ and
$2^{-31} \scale$ for the higher orders, whose initial step is $\scale / 4$; exactly at an edge, use
\code{Forward<P>} or \code{Backward<P>}. The stencil in the same example does not fail, since its default step of
$\epsmach^{1/3}$ never reaches zero, but its relative error is \num{\valEdgeCentralAtOneHundredthRelError}: the
truncation $h^2 / 3x^2$ with a step scaled by $\scale = 1$ rather than by $x$, the small-$\abs{x}$ case of
\cref{sec:rounding-scale}, which Ridders handles by adapting the step.

\paragraph{A pole between the samples, or aliasing.} The samples can be finite and still lie: a pole of $f$
between two sample points gives finite differences that converge to nothing, and a function that oscillates faster
than the initial step can vanish at every sample. The method has a test for a result that has at most two correct
bits by its own estimate while that estimate is far above rounding, and restarts below the initial step when it
fires (\cref{sb:aliasing}). It cannot see what no sample shows.

\paragraph{A function less accurate than $\epsmach$.} The floor assumes that \code{fn} is accurate to about
$\epsmach$. A function that carries the noise $\noise$ of an iterative solver or a fitted table
(\cref{sec:rounding-noise}) breaks that assumption, and the estimate becomes optimistic: measured over the same
\valRiddersCases{} random points with the values perturbed by $\noise = 10^{-8}$, the coverage falls to
\SI{\valNoiseEightRiddersCoverage}{\percent} with a worst ratio of \num{\valNoiseEightRiddersWorstRatio}
(\cref{fig:noise}). Pass an initial step on the scale of the noise, and read the estimate as a lower bound.

\paragraph{A kink inside the span.} A range break or a kink within $h_0$ of $x$ does not produce a non-finite
value, and Ridders returns a value for the wrong function. What it also returns is a large estimate, since the
base differences then converge like $\order(h)$ rather than $\order(h^2)$ and the entries keep changing: on the
thread's own range break the stencils return errors of order one in silence, while Ridders' relative estimate is
\num{\valKinkInsideRiddersRelEstimate}, eleven orders above its usual value (\cref{sec:using-pitfalls},
\cref{fig:kink}). The estimate flags it; the stencil does not.

returns two more digits and an estimate.

\section{Stencil or Ridders}\label{sec:ridders-choice}

Why does only Ridders estimate its error? A stencil could bound its truncation error only with extra evaluations,
at a second step or a higher order, and its rounding part alone is smallest exactly where truncation is largest;
an estimate that costs nothing would be worth nothing. So the library leaves a stencil's \code{abs_error} empty,
and a code that needs an estimate asks for Ridders. The choice follows from that. Inside an iteration, where the
derivative is recomputed many times and a Newton step tolerates an approximate slope, a stencil with the default
step is the right tool: two or four evaluations, eleven to thirteen digits, no decisions. For a value that is
reported, compared or published, Ridders: several times the evaluations, two more digits, and a number that says
how far to trust it. A stencil result can still be checked after the fact, by repeating it at another step or order,
or by Ridders itself (\cref{sec:using-check}).

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:difference-form]{Burden's difference form of the tableau}
The textbook writes column $j$ as the weighted combination $(4^j \Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}) / (4^j - 1)$.
The library computes the algebraically equal
\[
  \Aij{i}{j} = \Aij{i}{j-1} + \frac{\Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1},
\]
a correction added to the left neighbour, for three reasons. When the two parents agree exactly, the correction
is 0 and the entry equals them bit for bit, which is what the stop on exact agreement relies on; the product form
could round. The correction is a small quantity divided by a number above 1, so it cannot overflow where the
entries themselves are finite, whereas $4^j \Aij{i}{j-1}$ can exceed the largest finite value in float from
$j = 7$ on for a derivative near $2^{115}$; the library still checks the one subtraction and the one addition
before forming them, since two finite entries of opposite sign can differ by more than the largest value, and
treats a failure as a failed level. And only the previous row is needed: the tableau is two arrays of
\valRiddersLevelCap{} entries, filled from \code{T(0)} so that the real type need not be default-constructible,
and swapped after each level. The factors $4^j$ are built by repeated multiplication in the real type, exact in
binary; in a decimal type $4^j - 1$ rounds at large $j$ by at most half an epsilon, which does not matter.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:floor-coverage]{How Ridders stops, and how often its estimate holds}
\emph{The rules}, as the code has them. Level $i$ is the \code{Central<2>} rule for the order at $h_i = h_0 /
2^i$; before it is evaluated the method checks that $h_i$ is positive, that $x \pm h_i$ differ from $x$ (and for
$f'''$ and $f''''$ that $x \pm 2 h_i$ differ from $x \pm h_i$), and that no point can overflow; at the resolution
of $x$ the tableau simply ends. Each extrapolated entry gets the estimate $e_{i,j}$ of \cref{eq:ridders-estimate}
with the floor $r_i$ of its level; for $f'$ the floor is the rounding of the two samples over their distance, for
$f''$ the same from the three samples and the two offsets, and for $f'''$ and $f''''$ the rounding of the samples
plus the rounding of the points, $\tfrac12 \epsmach (\abs{x} + 2h)\, \abs{f'} \sum_k \abs{w_k} / (\den h^{\dorder})$
with $f'$ taken from the samples at $x \pm h$, since those rules divide by the nominal step. The best entry is the
one with the smallest estimate since the last restart. The tableau stops when an entry equals its parent exactly
(nothing is left to learn; that entry is best, with the floor as its estimate), when two consecutive levels bring
no smaller estimate, at the resolution of $x$, or when the level budget runs out. The result is the best entry
with $\errest = \max(e_{\mathrm{best}}, \abs{A_{\mathrm{best}} - \Aij{i}{i}})$ for the latest diagonal entry; it
needs at least two successful levels since the last restart.

\emph{Restarts.} A level fails if \code{fn} is not finite at a point, if a difference, quotient or tableau entry
would overflow, or if the points would; the tableau, its best entry and the stall counter are discarded and a
new tableau starts from half the failing step. A result is untrusted if $\errest \ge \abs{A_{\mathrm{best}}} /
4$, at most two correct bits, and $\errest / 64 > r_{\mathrm{best}}$, more than rounding explains; the method then
restarts from half the initial step and keeps the untrusted result as a fallback, returned if the budget runs out
before a trusted one appears. A tableau that ended at the resolution of $x$ is returned as it is, since no restart
could go lower; a derivative that is exactly 0 has an estimate near its floor and is not affected. The budget is
\valRiddersLevelCap{} levels in all; for the even orders $f(x)$ is evaluated once and kept across restarts, and if
it is not finite the result is \code{non_finite} after that one evaluation.

\emph{Where the constants come from.} The ratio 2, the two-level stall, the factor 1 on the floor, the factors 4
and 64 of the untrusted test and the cap of 32 were chosen with a prototype on random cases, and the design record
in the repository, \code{docs/PHASE3_API.md} and \code{docs/PHASE4_API.md}, gives the evidence for each: without
the floor the estimate fell below the actual error in more than half of the prototype's cases; a one-level stall
let a Runge-function case stop early; the factor 1 instead of 4 left poles between samples uncovered; factors
between 16 and 256 over the floor gave identical results. The book's own evidence is the study of
\cref{fig:ridders-coverage}: \valRiddersFunctions{} functions ($\mathrm{e}^x$, $\sin x$, $\log x$, $\sqrt{x}$,
$1/x$, the Runge function $1/(1 + 25x^2)$, $x^5$, $\mathrm{e}^{-x^2}$ and $\tanh x$) at
\valRiddersPointsPerFunction{} random points each in $[0.2, 3]$, with the default initial step, no noise and no
singularity within $h_0$ for $f'$. Measured: coverage \SI{\valRiddersCoverageFirst}{\percent} for $f'$
(\valRiddersUncoveredFirst{} of \valRiddersCases{} cases uncovered) and \SI{\valRiddersCoverageSecond}{\percent}
for $f''$ (\valRiddersUncoveredSecond{}); worst ratios of error to estimate \num{\valRiddersWorstRatioFirst} and
\num{\valRiddersWorstRatioSecond}; median ratios \num{\valRiddersMedianRatioFirst} and
\num{\valRiddersMedianRatioSecond}; \valRiddersExactCasesFirst{} and \valRiddersExactCasesSecond{} exact stops,
all on $x^5$; no restarts for $f'$ and \valRiddersRestartedSecond{} for $f''$, which are the cases of $\log x$ and
$\sqrt{x}$ below $x = 0.25$, where $h_0 = \scale / 4$ reaches below zero and the first levels are NaN. $1/x$ at
the same points does not restart: its pole lies between finite samples, the first levels are simply wrong, and the
tableau takes more levels and converges from the later ones, accurately. The tests hold every case to an actual
error within six times its estimate; the study's worst is \num{\valRiddersWorstRatioFirst}.
\end{sidebar}

\begin{sidebar}[label=sb:aliasing]{Aliasing: when the samples lie}
Take $f(x) = \sin(64 \pi x)$ at $x = 0$, whose derivative is $64\pi$. The default initial step is $1/32$, so the
first level samples $\sin(\pm 2\pi)$ and the second $\sin(\pm \pi)$: every sample is zero up to rounding, the two
base differences nearly agree, and the first extrapolated entry is a confident 0 with a tiny estimate.
The samples lie, and no formula applied to them can know it. What saves the call is the sequence: the third level
samples $\sin(\pm \pi / 2) = \pm 1$, and the diagonal entries jump to numbers of order 100 while the best entry
stays near 0. The final check raises the estimate to the distance from the latest diagonal entry, the untrusted
test (at most two correct bits, far above the floor) fires, and the method restarts from half the initial step;
the second tableau converges to $64\pi$, and the tests check that it does so to within a few units of rounding,
in about two dozen evaluations.
The design record names the case that cannot be caught: samples that are exactly periodic \emph{and} lie on a
polynomial, such as $\cos(64 \pi x)$ at 0 for $f''$, where every sample of the first levels is exactly 1, two
entries agree exactly, and the method stops with the floor as its estimate. That fools every sampling method,
not only this one; the remedy is an initial step on the function's own scale, which here is the period.
\end{sidebar}
