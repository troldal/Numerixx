\chapter{Derivations}\label{ch:derivations}

This appendix proves what the sidebars of \cref{ch:stencils,ch:orders,ch:ridders} only cite. It answers four
questions, one per section. Why do the moment conditions decide a rule's order and its error constant? Why does the
Lagrange formula produce the weights? Why does Richardson's tableau work, and what does its error estimate measure?
And where do the best step and its constant come from?

Three assumptions hold throughout. First, $f$ has as many continuous derivatives as the expansions use, and one more
bounded derivative where the error model's $\order(h^{\aorder + 1})$ is claimed (\cref{sec:derivations-moments}).
Second, the nodes $n_k$ are distinct integers. Third, the weights $w_k$ are the rational numbers of
\cref{eq:stencils-rule}. With the library's integer numerators $c_k = \den w_k$ instead, every moment is multiplied
by $\den$.

\section{Taylor expansions and the moment conditions}\label{sec:derivations-moments}

We start from Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder (Burden et al.,\cite{burden2016} Section 1.1). For each
node, with $x_k = x + n_k h$, it gives
\[
  f(x_k) = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{f^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\, n_k^j h^j + \frac{f^{(m)}(\xi_k)}{m!}\, n_k^m h^m,
  \qquad \xi_k \text{ between } x \text{ and } x_k .
\]
Multiply by $w_k$ and sum over the nodes. In the term with $h^j$, the factor $\sum_k w_k n_k^j$ appears. This is
the moment $\mom{j} = \sum_k w_k n_k^j$ of \cref{eq:stencils-moments}, so the powers of $h$ collect into the
moments:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_k w_k f(x_k) = \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{\mom{j}}{j!}\, h^j f^{(j)}(x) + \frac{h^m}{m!} \sum_k w_k n_k^m f^{(m)}(\xi_k).
  \label{eq:derivations-taylor}
\end{equation}
Now suppose the moment conditions hold up to $m - 1 = \dorder + \aorder - 1$. That is, $\mom{\dorder} = \dorder!$,
and $\mom{j} = 0$ for every other $j$ below $m$. In the sum over $j$, only the term with $j = \dorder$ survives, and
since $\mom{\dorder} / \dorder! = 1$ it is $h^{\dorder} f^{(\dorder)}(x)$. So the sum on the left is
$h^{\dorder} f^{(\dorder)}(x)$ plus the remainder, and dividing by $h^{\dorder}$ gives
\[
  \frac{1}{h^{\dorder}} \sum_k w_k f(x_k) = f^{(\dorder)}(x) + \frac{h^{\aorder}}{(\dorder + \aorder)!}
  \sum_k w_k n_k^{\dorder + \aorder} f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(\xi_k).
\]
The remainder is $\order(h^{\aorder})$, which is the accuracy order. We can say more. Each $\xi_k$ tends to $x$ with
$h$, and $f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}$ is continuous. So each $f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(\xi_k)$ tends to
$f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(x)$, and the weights that are left, $\sum_k w_k n_k^{\dorder + \aorder}$, add up to the moment
$\mom{\dorder + \aorder}$. The remainder is therefore $\mom{\dorder + \aorder}\, h^{\aorder}
f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(x) / (\dorder + \aorder)! + o(h^{\aorder})$, for every
rule.\mnote{$o(h^{\aorder})$, ``little o of $h^{\aorder}$'': terms whose ratio to $h^{\aorder}$ tends to 0 as $h$
tends to 0.} The correction is $\order(h^{\aorder + 1})$ when $f$ has one more bounded derivative, which the error
model assumes throughout. This asymptotic form is what the error model uses, with the error constant
\begin{equation}
  \errc = \frac{\abs{\mom{\dorder + \aorder}}}{(\dorder + \aorder)!}, \qquad
  \text{or } \frac{\abs{\mom{\dorder + \aorder}}}{(\dorder + \aorder)!\,\den} \text{ with the integer numerators,}
  \label{eq:derivations-errc}
\end{equation}
as \cref{ch:tables} tabulates it.

The sharper statement with a single intermediate point, as in \cref{eq:stencils-central-four} and
\cref{eq:orders-second}, needs one more argument. Which argument depends on the rule. Suppose first that the products
$w_k n_k^{\dorder + \aorder}$ all have one sign. Then the sum in the remainder is their total,
$\mom{\dorder + \aorder}$, times a weighted mean of the values $f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(\xi_k)$. A continuous function
takes every value between its smallest and its largest, so it takes that mean at some point: the intermediate-value
theorem collapses the sum above to one point. The remainder is
$\mom{\dorder + \aorder}\, h^{\aorder} f^{(\dorder + \aorder)}(\xi) / (\dorder + \aorder)!$ for some $\xi$ in the span
of the stencil. That is the case for \cref{eq:orders-second}, whose products are $1, 0, 1$. It is not the case for
\cref{eq:stencils-central-four}, whose products $-\tfrac83, \tfrac23, \tfrac23, -\tfrac83$ have mixed signs, nor for
any central rule for $f'$ above order 2, nor for some one-sided rules.

For the first derivative, the single-point form follows instead from the interpolation error formula, as Burden et
al.\cite{burden2016} (Section 4.1) derive the five-point formulas. Put $x$ itself among the nodes: where the rule
omits it, add it with the weight 0, which changes nothing. Then $f(x + th) - p(t)$ equals
$f^{(m)}(\xi(t)) \prod_j (t - n_j)\, h^m / m!$. Now differentiate once at $t = 0$. Since 0 is a node, the node
product vanishes there. So, by the product rule, only the term that differentiates the node product is left. The
derivative of the node product at $t = 0$ is the product of its other factors there. On the left, the derivative in
$t$ of $f(x + th)$ brings a factor $h$, so the left side becomes $h f'(x) - p'(0)$. Dividing by $h$ leaves $f'(x) - p'(0)/h = f^{(m)}(\xi)\, h^{m-1} \prod_{n_j \ne 0} (-n_j) / m!$, with one $\xi$. For
\code{Central<4>} the product over the nodes $-2, -1, 1, 2$ is $4$, and $m = 5$ with the added node 0. Since
$4 / 5! = 1/30$, this gives the $+ h^4 f^{(5)}(\xi) / 30$ of \cref{eq:stencils-central-four}. The one-sided rules,
which contain $x$ as a node, are covered by the same formula. For the higher derivatives and for mixed signs, the
book uses only the asymptotic form.

Now take a symmetric stencil: the nodes come in pairs $\pm n$, with $w_{-n} = (-1)^{\dorder} w_n$. A pair
contributes $w_n n^j + w_{-n} (-n)^j = w_n n^j (1 + (-1)^{\dorder + j})$ to $\mom{j}$, which is zero when
$j - \dorder$ is odd. So every moment $\mom{j}$ with $j - \dorder$ odd vanishes identically. The expansion of a
central rule therefore contains only the powers $h^{\aorder}, h^{\aorder + 2}, \ldots$: an even expansion in $h$.
This is what \cref{sb:remainder} states for the central difference, and what the extrapolation of
\cref{sec:derivations-richardson} relies on for every order. It also explains why a central rule needs one node fewer
than a one-sided rule of the same order. Of the $\dorder + \aorder$ conditions, those with $j - \dorder$ odd hold by
symmetry, and cost no weight.

Rounding enters \cref{eq:derivations-taylor} through the samples. Suppose each $f(x_k)$ is off by at most
$\epsmach \abs{f(x_k)}$. Then the weighted sum is off by at most $\sum_k \abs{w_k}\, \epsmach \abs{f(x_k)}$, by the
triangle inequality. For samples of about the size of $\abs{f(x)}$, this is $\ampl \epsmach \abs{f}$, with
$\ampl = \sum_k \abs{w_k}$. The division by $h^{\dorder}$ turns it into the rounding term
$\ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / h^{\dorder}$ of \cref{eq:orders-model}. The points are rounded too: each $x_k$ is off by up
to $\epsmach \abs{x_k} / 2$. That moves each sample by about $\epsmach \abs{x f'} / 2$, and adds the third term named
in \cref{sec:stencils-price}.

\section{The Lagrange formula for the weights}\label{sec:derivations-lagrange}

Let $p$ be the polynomial of degree below the number of nodes $m$ that interpolates the samples. We write it in the
scaled variable $t$, with the points at $x + t h$: $p(t) = \sum_k f(x_k) L_k(t)$. Here the Lagrange basis polynomial
$L_k(t) = \prod_{j \ne k} (t - n_j) / (n_k - n_j)$ is 1 at $t = n_k$, where every factor is 1, and 0 at every other
node, where one factor is 0. So $p$ takes the value $f(x_k)$ at each node. We differentiate $p$ $\dorder$ times at
$t = 0$, and divide by $h^{\dorder}$, since each derivative in $t$ carries a factor $h$. This gives an estimate of
$f^{(\dorder)}(x)$ of the form \cref{eq:stencils-rule}, with
\begin{equation}
  w_k = L_k^{(\dorder)}(0) = \dorder!\,[t^{\dorder}]\, L_k(t),
  \label{eq:derivations-lagrange}
\end{equation}
that is, the coefficient of $t^{\dorder}$ in $L_k$, times $\dorder!$. This is what the library's generator computes,
with exact integer arithmetic (Burden et al.,\cite{burden2016} Section 4.1).

These weights satisfy the moment conditions, for the following reason. Interpolation on $m$ nodes reproduces every
polynomial of degree below $m$. Suppose $f(x + t h) = t^j$ with $j < m$. Then $p(t) = t^j$ exactly, since both sides
are polynomials of degree below $m$ that agree at $m$ points. (Their difference has degree below $m$ and $m$ roots,
so it is zero.) For that $f$ the samples are $f(x_k) = n_k^j$, so $\sum_k w_k n_k^j = p^{(\dorder)}(0) =
(t^j)^{(\dorder)}(0)$. This is $\dorder!$ for $j = \dorder$, and 0 for every other $j < m$. These are exactly the
moment conditions up to $\mom{m-1}$. So the Lagrange weights are the unique solution of that linear system, and the
rule's order is at least $m - \dorder$. For the one-sided rules, $m = \aorder + \dorder$, and the order is exactly
$\aorder$, since $\mom{m}$ does not vanish. For the central rules, $m = \aorder + \dorder - 1$, and the symmetry
argument of \cref{sec:derivations-moments} supplies the missing condition $\mom{m} = 0$.

The library's own worked example is \code{Central<4>} for $f'$, on the nodes $-2, -1, 1, 2$. Take the node
$n_k = -1$. The numerator of $L_k$ is $(t + 2)(t - 1)(t - 2) = t^3 - t^2 - 4t + 4$, and its denominator is
$(-1 + 2)(-1 - 1)(-1 - 2) = 6$. The numerator's coefficient of $t^1$ is $-4$, so $w_{-1} = 1! \cdot (-4) / 6 = -2/3$.
The other three nodes give $1/12$, $2/3$ and $-1/12$. Over the common denominator 12, the numerators are
$1, -8, 8, -1$. This is the table that the tests check against Fornberg's.\cite{fornberg1988} The same computation
for Chapra's backward rule for the fourth derivative, on the nodes $-5, \ldots, 0$, gives $-2, 11, -24, 26, -14, 3$
over 1. This is the row that the tests compare with the published one (Chapra, Chapter 23).\cite{chapra2015}

\section{Richardson extrapolation}\label{sec:derivations-richardson}

Let $\Dc{h}$ be a base rule with an even expansion, $\Dc{h} = f^{(\dorder)}(x) + a_2 h^2 + a_4 h^4 + \cdots$. By
\cref{sec:derivations-moments}, every \code{Central<2>} rule has one. With $h_i = h_0 / 2^i$ and $D_i = \Dc{h_i}$,
define $\Aij{i}{0} = D_i$ and
\begin{equation}
  \Aij{i}{j} = \frac{4^j \Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1}
             = \Aij{i}{j-1} + \frac{\Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}}{4^j - 1}.
  \label{eq:derivations-tableau}
\end{equation}
The claim is that column $j$ has an expansion starting at $h^{2j + 2}$ (Burden et al.,\cite{burden2016} Section
4.2). We prove it by induction on $j$. Suppose that $\Aij{i}{j-1} = f^{(\dorder)} + c\, h_i^{2j} +
\order(h_i^{2j+2})$, with a coefficient $c$ that does not depend on $i$. This holds for $j = 1$ with $c = a_2$, since
column 0 is the base rule itself. The parent one level up has the step $2 h_i$, and $(2 h_i)^{2j} = 4^j h_i^{2j}$.
So $\Aij{i-1}{j-1} = f^{(\dorder)} + c\, 4^j h_i^{2j} + \order(h_i^{2j+2})$. In the combination
$4^j \Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}$, the $h^{2j}$ terms cancel exactly, since both are $4^j c\, h_i^{2j}$. The
constant term is $(4^j - 1) f^{(\dorder)}$. Dividing by $4^j - 1$ leaves $f^{(\dorder)} + \order(h_i^{2j+2})$, again
with a leading coefficient independent of $i$. The two forms in \cref{eq:derivations-tableau} are equal by a line of
algebra: write $4^j = (4^j - 1) + 1$ in the first numerator. The second form is the one that the library computes
(\cref{sb:difference-form}).

The a posteriori estimate, the one computed from the results themselves, follows from the same expansion. We have
$\Aij{i}{j-1} - f^{(\dorder)} = c\, h_i^{2j} + \cdots$ and $\Aij{i-1}{j-1} - f^{(\dorder)} = 4^j c\, h_i^{2j} +
\cdots$. By the claim just proved, $\Aij{i}{j} - f^{(\dorder)}$ is only $\order(h_i^{2j+2})$. Hence
\[
  \Aij{i}{j} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}
  = \bigl( \Aij{i}{j} - f^{(\dorder)} \bigr) - \bigl( \Aij{i-1}{j-1} - f^{(\dorder)} \bigr)
  = -4^j c\, h_i^{2j} + \order(h_i^{2j+2}),
\]
so the change of an entry from its parent equals the parent's error, to leading order. And the parent's error is of
a lower order in the step than the entry's own: $h_i^{2j}$ against $h_i^{2j+2}$. While truncation dominates, the
change is therefore a conservative estimate of the entry's error. Once rounding dominates, it is not. The entries of
a converged column are then the limit plus independent rounding, of about the size of the rounding floor. The
smallest of several such differences underestimates the error. This is why \cref{eq:ridders-estimate} takes the
larger of the change and the floor, and why Ridders' method stops once two levels bring no smaller estimate.

The one-sided forms of \cref{sec:ridders-onesided} extrapolate the \code{Forward<1>} rule of order $\dorder$. Its
nodes $0, \ldots, \dorder$ have no symmetry, so its expansion $f^{(\dorder)} + b_1 h + b_2 h^2 + \cdots$ has every
power of $h$. The same induction works, with $\Aij{i}{j-1} = f^{(\dorder)} + c\, h_i^{j} + \order(h_i^{j+1})$ and
the parent $f^{(\dorder)} + c\, 2^j h_i^{j} + \cdots$. It shows that $(2^j \Aij{i}{j-1} - \Aij{i-1}{j-1}) / (2^j - 1)$
cancels the term in $h^j$ and leaves $\order(h_i^{j+1})$. So the divisor is $2^j - 1$, and each column gains one
order (Burden et al.,\cite{burden2016} Section 4.2).

What about rounding? Unrolled, an entry is a combination $\sum_k \gamma_k D_k$ of the base values. So, to first
order, it inherits at most $\sum_k \abs{\gamma_k} r_k$ of rounding. The $\gamma_k$ alternate in sign, so the
subtraction in each step of the tableau combines coefficients of the same sign, and no two contributions cancel. As a
result, the recursion $r_{i,j} = (2^j r_{i,j-1} + r_{i-1,j-1}) / (2^j - 1)$ computes that sum exactly.

\section{The error model and the best step}\label{sec:derivations-model}

The error model of \cref{eq:orders-model} is the sum of two terms: the leading truncation term, and the rounding
term of \cref{sec:derivations-moments}. We write $F = \abs{f^{(\aorder + \dorder)}(x)}$ for short:
\[
  E(h) = \errc\, h^{\aorder} F + \ampl\, \epsmach \frac{\abs{f}}{h^{\dorder}} .
\]
It is convex in $h > 0$, since $h^{\aorder}$ and $1/h^{\dorder}$ both are, so its minimum lies where its derivative
vanishes. The derivative vanishes where
$\aorder \errc\, h^{\aorder - 1} F = \dorder \ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / h^{\dorder + 1}$, that is at
\begin{equation}
  \hbest = \left( \frac{\dorder \ampl\, \epsmach \abs{f}}{\aorder \errc\, F} \right)^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} .
  \label{eq:derivations-best-step}
\end{equation}
Multiply the condition for the minimum by $h$. It then says that $\aorder$ times the truncation term equals
$\dorder$ times the rounding term. So at the minimum the two terms stand in the ratio $\dorder : \aorder$
(truncation to rounding), and $E(\hbest) = (1 + \dorder / \aorder)\, \ampl \epsmach \abs{f} / {\hbest}^{\dorder}$.
Since ${\hbest}^{\dorder}$ is proportional to $\epsmach^{\dorder/(\aorder + \dorder)}$, this is of order
$\epsmach^{1 - \dorder/(\aorder + \dorder)} = \epsmach^{\aorder/(\aorder + \dorder)}$, times a factor built from
$\abs{f}$, $F$ and the constants. The default step assumes that $f$ varies on the scale $\scale$. That means
$\abs{f^{(k)}} \approx \abs{f} / \scale^k$, so that $\abs{f} / F \approx \scale^{\aorder + \dorder}$. Then
$\hbest \approx \kappa\, \epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} \scale$, with the constant
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \left( \frac{\dorder \ampl}{\aorder \errc} \right)^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)},
  \label{eq:derivations-constant}
\end{equation}
which the library drops: its step is $\epsmach^{1/(\aorder + \dorder)} \scale$ (\cref{eq:orders-step}).

The cost of dropping $\kappa$ is bounded by the model. Take a step $h = \hbest / \kappa$, which is the library's step
under the model's assumption. The truncation term, proportional to $h^{\aorder}$, is then $\kappa^{-\aorder}$ times
its value at $\hbest$. The rounding term, proportional to $1/h^{\dorder}$, is $\kappa^{\dorder}$ times its value. By
the ratio $\dorder : \aorder$, those values are the fractions $\dorder / (\aorder + \dorder)$ and
$\aorder / (\aorder + \dorder)$ of $E(\hbest)$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \frac{E(\hbest / \kappa)}{E(\hbest)} = \frac{\dorder\, \kappa^{-\aorder} + \aorder\, \kappa^{\dorder}}{\aorder + \dorder} .
  \label{eq:derivations-cost}
\end{equation}
Now the numbers. For the first derivative, $\kappa$ lies between \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFirst} and 2. The
lower end is \code{Central<2>}, where $\dorder = 1$, $\aorder = 2$, $\ampl = 1$ and $\errc = \tfrac16$ make it
$3^{1/3}$. The upper end is \code{Forward<1>}, where $\aorder = 1$, $\ampl = 2$ and $\errc = \tfrac12$ make it
$(2 \cdot 2)^{1/2}$. \Cref{eq:derivations-cost} gives at most \num{\valStepCostCentralTenFirst}, reached by
\code{Central<10>} with $\kappa = \num{\valStepConstantCentralTenFirst}$. For \code{Central<2>} and $f'$ it is
\num{\valStepCostCentralTwoFirst}. For the higher orders, $\kappa$ is \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoSecond} for
$f''$ and \num{\valStepConstantCentralTwoFourth} for $f''''$ with \code{Central<2>}. The cost by
\cref{eq:derivations-cost} is then \num{\valStepCostCentralTwoSecond} and \num{\valStepCostCentralTwoFourth}. For
\code{Central<4>} and $f''''$ it is \num{\valStepCostCentralFourFourth}, the largest of the four rules that the design
record quotes. These agree with the design record's figures of 3.5, 11 and 15 (\code{docs/deriv/PHASE4_API.md}). The
library's documentation gives the bound for $f'$ as about 1.6, and \num{\valStepCostCentralTenFirst} for
\code{Central<10>}.

These are model figures, for a function that varies on the scale $\scale$ exactly. The design record's prototype
(\code{docs/deriv/PHASE4_API.md}) reports the opposite experience on nine test functions that vary faster than
$\scale$. There, half the default step was better for every order, by a factor 1.2 to 1.8 for \code{Central<2>}.
That is why the library keeps the plain convention of \cref{eq:orders-step} for every order, and states its
sensitivity instead (\cref{sb:step-constant}, \cref{sb:fourth-sensitivity}).
