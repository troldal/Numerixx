\chapter{Floating-point essentials}\label{ch:floating-point}

This appendix collects the facts about floating-point arithmetic that \cref{ch:rounding} and the sidebars use. The
standard references are Higham's book and Goldberg's survey;\cite{higham2002,goldberg1991} the formats and the
rounding rule are fixed by IEEE~754.\cite{ieee754-2019}

\section{Representation}\label{sec:fp-representation}

A binary floating-point number is
\begin{equation*}
  x = \pm m \cdot 2^{e}, \qquad 1 \le m < 2,
\end{equation*}
where the \emph{significand} $m$ has $p$ binary digits and the \emph{exponent} $e$ is an integer in a fixed range.
For \code{float} $p = \num{\valDigitsFloat}$, for \code{double} $p = \num{\valDigitsDouble}$ (\cref{tab:fp-constants}). Every
integer up to $2^{p}$ is exact, which is why the library's integer weights, all below $2^{24}$, are exact in every
type with at least 24 significant bits. Between two powers of two the numbers are evenly spaced; at each power of two
the spacing doubles.

\section{Machine epsilon, the unit roundoff and the ulp}\label{sec:fp-epsilon}

\emph{Machine epsilon} $\epsmach = 2^{1-p}$ is the distance from 1 to the next larger number: $2^{-52}$ for
\code{double}. The spacing at a general $x$ is its \emph{unit in the last place}, $\ulp(x) = \epsmach \cdot 2^{e}$
for $2^{e} \le \abs{x} < 2^{e+1}$. It is proportional to $\abs{x}$ within a factor of two: $\ulp(1) =
\num{\valUlpAtOne}$, just below 1 the spacing is \num{\valUlpBelowOne}, and $\ulp(1000) = \num{\valUlpAtOneThousand}$.

IEEE~754 arithmetic rounds the exact result of each operation $+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$ and $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ to the
nearest representable number (ties to an even last digit). The error is at most half a spacing, so
\begin{equation*}
  \fl(a \circ b) = (a \circ b)(1 + \delta), \qquad \abs{\delta} \le \epsmach / 2,
\end{equation*}
as long as the result is neither too large nor too small for the format. The bound $\epsmach / 2$ is the \emph{unit
roundoff}, which many texts call $u$. This book speaks of $\epsmach$ only, and the library's rounding model uses it:
a value of $f$ that is the result of many operations is rarely accurate to half an ulp, and $\epsmach \abs{f}$ is a
more honest estimate of its error.

\section{The ends of the range}\label{sec:fp-range}

The largest finite number is $\fmax$. A result beyond it rounds to infinity, and infinity minus infinity is NaN, not a
number. The library checks what could overflow (before it is formed; the final quotient after) and reports
\code{error::non_finite} instead; it never calls \code{fn} at a point that is not finite.

At the other end, below the smallest normal number (\num{\valMinDouble} for \code{double}) the exponent can fall no
further, and the numbers continue with a fixed spacing equal to the smallest subnormal, \num{\valDenormMinDouble}.
These \emph{subnormal} numbers fill the gap to zero gradually, but their relative accuracy falls as they shrink:
rounding there is absolute, not relative. The library's rounding model accounts for that: the rounding error of a
nonzero value of $f$ is $\epsmach \abs{f}$, but never less than the smallest subnormal.

\section{Why $x + h$ is not $x + h$}\label{sec:fp-step}

A finite difference evaluates $f$ at $x + h$, and that sum is rounded like any other. The function therefore sees
$\fl(x + h)$, which differs from $x + h$ by up to half the spacing at the result: $\ulp(x) / 2$, or $\ulp(x)$ when
$x + h$ crosses a power of two (the two-sided spacing of \cref{sb:ulp}). Two consequences follow, both met in
\cref{ch:rounding}. A step below $\ulp(x) / 2$ does not move $x$. If neither $x + h$ nor $x - h$ moves, the library
reports \code{error::non_finite}. If only one moves, as can happen near a power of two, a two-node rule for $f'$
returns a finite value that is mostly rounding, and every other rule reports \code{non_finite}
(\cref{sb:ulp,sec:api-derivative}). Hence the requirement: a step well above $\ulp(x)$. And for any step the distance
between the two points of the central difference is not exactly $2h$, which is why the two-node rules divide by the
distance actually evaluated (\cref{sb:actual-distance}).

\section{Cancellation}\label{sec:fp-cancellation}

The subtraction of two nearby numbers of the same sign is exact: if $a, b > 0$ and $a / 2 \le b \le 2a$, then
$\fl(a - b) = a - b$ (Sterbenz's lemma, Theorem 4.3.1; likewise for $a, b < 0$).\cite{sterbenz1974} The danger is not in the subtraction but in its inputs.
If $a$ and $b$ carry errors $\delta_a$ and $\delta_b$ from earlier rounding, the difference carries
$\delta_a - \delta_b$, and its relative error is
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\abs{\delta_a - \delta_b}}{\abs{a - b}} \le \frac{\abs{\delta_a} + \abs{\delta_b}}{\abs{a - b}},
\end{equation*}
which is large when $a$ and $b$ are close: an error that was small compared with $a$ and $b$ is large compared with
their difference. This is \emph{cancellation}, and it is the whole reason for the rounding branch of the error model:
the values $f(x + h)$ and $f(x - h)$ come closer as $h$ shrinks, their rounding errors do not, and the division by
$2h$ magnifies what is left (\cref{sec:rounding-v}).

\section{Other types}\label{sec:fp-types}

Nothing above is special to binary: decimal types round to decimal digits, with their own $\epsmach$, and
multiprecision types such as \code{cpp_bin_float_50} of Boost.Multiprecision ($p = \num{\valDigitsMultiprecision}$
bits, $\epsmach = \num{\valEpsMultiprecision}$) follow the same rules with more digits. What the library needs from a
type, its arithmetic, its comparisons and a few members of \code{std::numeric_limits}, is listed in
\cref{sec:api-real}; its error model needs only $\epsmach$.
